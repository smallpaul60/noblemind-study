*Chapter 3*

# Conditional Statements About Obedience (New Testament)

The previous chapter ended on a sentence that leans across the gap between the Testaments. *He has not changed. We should not expect Him to.* If that is true — if the God who related to His people through stated conditions through the entire Old Testament is the God who relates to His people now — then what should we expect to find in the New Testament? We should expect to find Him doing the same thing.

We do.

The conditional structure of God's covenant speech does not stop at Malachi and resume in altered form on the other side of the four hundred silent years. It runs straight through. The same *if*, the same *whoever*, the same *unless*, the same *when*, the same warnings, the same promises set on the same kind of conditions — but now in Greek instead of Hebrew, on the lips of Jesus rather than Moses, in the letters of Paul and Peter and John instead of the scrolls of the prophets, addressed to a people gathered into Christ instead of gathered at Sinai. The vocabulary changes; the structure does not. The Mediator is new. The conditioner is not.

This chapter walks through the texts that show it. They come from nearly every voice in the New Testament canon — Jesus Himself, Paul in five letters, John in his Gospel and his epistle, the writer of Hebrews, James. They are not difficult texts, individually. Each one says, in plain language, that some response on the part of the hearer is the condition of the promise. The difficulty is not in any single passage. The difficulty is in seeing what they all together say to the modern reader who has been taught that the New Covenant is the place where conditional structure ends. They say it does not.

## Two Keys: Galatians 3:25 and Malachi 3:6

Before walking the texts, two passages set the framework, and the rest of the chapter rests on them.

The first is Galatians 3:24–25. Paul has been arguing through Galatians 3 that justification is by faith, not by works of the Law of Moses, and that the Law was added "because of transgressions" (v. 19) until the Seed — Christ — should come. Then he says this:

> Therefore the Law has become our tutor to lead us to Christ, so that we may be justified by faith. But now that faith has come, we are no longer under a tutor. (Gal. 3:24–25)

The word translated "tutor" here is not what we mean by tutor today. In Paul's world, it described a household servant entrusted with the supervision of a child — walking him to school, watching his conduct, disciplining him when needed, restraining his impulses, keeping him on the path. The role had a definite end-point. The household tutor did not own the child. He did not save the child. He brought the child to the place where he was ready for what came next, and then his job was done.

That is what the Law of Moses was. Not a permanent dwelling, but the household servant who kept the child on the path until the Mediator arrived. The Law's holiness was real; its commands were holy and righteous and good (Rom. 7:12); the conditional structure in which it operated was God's structure. But the Law's role was a role with an end. When Christ came, the household tutor was dismissed. The child reached his majority. The new relationship of son to Father in Christ replaced the relationship of pupil to guardian.

This is why a Christian is not under the Law of Moses. He is not bound by the dietary laws, the sacrificial system, the Levitical priesthood, the circumcision requirement, the Sabbath ordinances. The writer of Hebrews makes this explicit: the priesthood has been changed (Heb. 7:12); the first covenant has been made obsolete (Heb. 8:13); the sacrifices of bulls and goats have been replaced by the once-for-all offering of Christ (Heb. 10:1–10). The administration is new.

What is not new is the God of the administration. And here Galatians 3:25 is sometimes pressed beyond what Paul says. Paul says we are no longer under a *tutor*. He does not say we are no longer under conditions. The Law as a household guardian has been dismissed. But Paul, in the same letter, refers to "the law of Christ" (Gal. 6:2), and elsewhere calls himself "not without the law of God but under the law of Christ" (1 Cor. 9:21). Christians are not under Moses. They are under Christ. The conditional structure that ran through the Mosaic administration runs equally — and in some respects more searchingly — through the apostolic administration, because the conditioner of both administrations is the same God.

That is the second key. Malachi 3:6:

> For I, the LORD, do not change.

Three witnesses, brought forward at the end of the previous chapter, set this declaration in three different settings: Psalm 102:25–27, James 1:17, and the New Testament's own counterpart in Hebrews 13:8 — "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever." Together they cover the Hebrew canon, the Gospel revelation, and the apostolic letters. The God who did not change in the days of Malachi did not change at the cross, did not change at Pentecost, did not change at the close of the New Testament canon, has not changed since. He is exactly the kind of God He has always been.

So if we found, in Chapter 2, that this God dealt with His people through stated conditions across fifteen Old Testament passages, the New Testament should not surprise us by replacing the structure with something altogether different. It does not. The pages that follow gather the texts that show it.

## The Words of Jesus

Begin where the New Testament begins — with the voice of Christ Himself. The four passages that follow are not gathered from obscure corners of the Gospels. They are familiar verses, taught for centuries from pulpits, often preached without anyone noticing how thoroughly conditional they are.

### Matthew 7:21

The Sermon on the Mount closes with a series of contrasts: two ways, two trees, two foundations, two kinds of confessor. In the middle of the section about confessors, Jesus says this:

> Not everyone who says to Me, "Lord, Lord," will enter the kingdom of heaven, but he who does the will of My Father who is in heaven will enter. (Matt. 7:21)

Two clauses, set against each other. *Not* everyone who says — but he who does. The condition for entering the kingdom is named, and the condition is doing. Saying "Lord, Lord" is not the condition. The text is not subtle about this. The very phrase a man would use to confess his Lordship over Christ — "Lord, Lord," repeated for emphasis — is named by Christ Himself as inadequate to enter, when it is unaccompanied by doing.

What is the kingdom of heaven? Matthew uses the phrase where Mark and Luke use "kingdom of God" (compare Matt. 13:31 with Mark 4:30–31), and the kingdom of God is, in the New Testament, the reign of Christ over His people. Jesus said it was "at hand" in His preaching (Matt. 4:17), said some standing with Him would not taste death until they saw it come "with power" (Mark 9:1), and by Acts 2 Peter was preaching that the risen Christ had been enthroned at God's right hand (Acts 2:30–36). Colossians 1:13 says God "rescued us from the domain of darkness, and transferred us to the kingdom of His beloved Son" — a present transfer accomplished in the lives of the Colossian Christians. The kingdom is the ruled people of Christ, entered now, consummated at His return. Entry into that ruled people is what Matthew 7:21 conditions on doing the will of the Father.

What is the will of the Father in a general sense? Jesus answers it in John 6:40: "this is the will of My Father, that everyone who beholds the Son and believes in Him will have eternal life." The Father's general will is faith in the Son for eternal life. But the believing of John 6 is not bare mental assent. In the same chapter Jesus calls it a "work" (v. 29), describes it as hearing and learning from the Father (v. 45), and presses it further with "unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink His blood, you have no life in yourselves" (v. 53) — a figure for active participation in Him. Matthew 7:21 itself supplies the qualifier the modern reader most needs to hear: it is not calling Jesus Lord that enters the kingdom, but doing the Father's will. The "belief" John 6:40 requires is the believing that obeys. Without obedience, "belief" in James 2:19 belongs to the demons.

### Matthew 16:24–26

A few chapters later, Jesus has just told His disciples for the first time that He must go to Jerusalem and be killed and raised. Peter has rebuked Him. Jesus has rebuked Peter. Then He turns to all the disciples and says:

> If anyone wishes to come after Me, he must deny himself, and take up his cross and follow Me. For whoever wishes to save his life will lose it; but whoever loses his life for My sake will find it. For what will it profit a man if he gains the whole world and forfeits his soul? Or what will a man give in exchange for his soul? (Matt. 16:24–26)

The structure here is *if anyone wishes…he must.* The wish is the implied condition; the action is the demand. Three actions, tied together: deny self, take up cross, follow Christ. They are not three options. They are one decision, made three ways.

To deny self is to renounce self-rule. It is to refuse to sit on the throne of one's own life. Paul stated the same thing positively in Galatians 2:20: "I have been crucified with Christ; and it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me." Luke's parallel adds the word "daily" — *let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow Me* (Luke 9:23). It is not a one-time gesture but a continuing posture. Romans 12:1–2 calls it presenting the body as a living sacrifice, transformed and not conformed to this world.

The tie to obedience is direct. If I am sovereign over my own life, I will obey myself — keeping the commands I find agreeable and negotiating the ones I do not. If Christ is sovereign, I obey whether I like the command or not. Denial of self is what makes real obedience possible. A person who has not denied himself will always find reasons not to obey the commands that cost him something.

Verse 25 explains why this is good news rather than misery. *Whoever wishes to save his life will lose it; but whoever loses his life for My sake will find it.* The man who clings to this present life as his highest good — its comforts, its reputation, its security, its length — loses the life that actually matters. The man who surrenders this present life for Christ's sake finds real life. Verse 26 drives the contrast home: a man who gains the world but forfeits his soul has gained nothing. The conditional structure of v. 24 — *if any man wishes…he must* — is not a hard saying laid alongside a softer offer. It is the gate to a kingdom whose currency is reversed from this present age.

### Matthew 19:17

A rich young man comes to Jesus with a question. "Teacher, what good thing shall I do that I may obtain eternal life?" Jesus's answer:

> If you wish to enter into life, keep the commandments. (Matt. 19:17)

This verse, by itself, ends any tidy slogan that says all a person has to do is believe in order to be saved. The young man asked what to *do* in order to obtain life. Jesus did not correct the verb. He did not say, "You don't do anything — just believe." He answered the question as asked: *if you wish to enter into life, keep the commandments.*

This does not mean Jesus was teaching salvation by flawless law-keeping. The rest of the account, vv. 20–22, shows that the man's heart problem surfaced precisely when Jesus put the commandment about possessions to him: "If you wish to be complete, go and sell your possessions and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow Me." The young man went away grieving, because he was unwilling to part with what he had. The point is not that the man could have earned eternal life by perfect obedience to the Decalogue. The point is that Jesus tied life to commandment-keeping, and that the test of whether a man would keep the commandments came down to whether his heart was on what God said or on what he himself preferred.

This is the Jesus who is alleged, in popular preaching, to have offered eternal life on the terms of bare belief. The Jesus of Matthew 19 ties entry to the keeping of commandments. The Jesus of John 6 calls believing a "work" and says no man comes to Him unless the Father teaches and draws him through that word. The two Jesuses are the same Jesus. He has not changed.

### Luke 6:46

Sometimes Jesus presses the contradiction of empty profession into a single rhetorical question. Luke 6:46 is one of those moments:

> Why do you call Me, "Lord, Lord," and do not do what I say? (Luke 6:46)

The word *Lord* itself means *master*. A master who is not obeyed is not, in any actual sense, being treated as master, however often the title is repeated. Calling Christ "Lord" while refusing His commands is not piety. It is a contradiction of the title.

The verses that follow drive the point in. The man who comes to Jesus, hears His words, and acts on them is like a man building on rock. The man who hears and does not act is like a man building on sand. Same coming. Same hearing. The difference is doing. When the storm comes — and the storm comes — the doing is what stands and the not-doing is what falls. This is the Lord whose New Testament Christians read.

## The Vine

Among all the metaphors Jesus chose for His relationship to His disciples, the one He used in His final extended teaching to the eleven was the most agrarian and the most exacting:

> Abide in Me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself unless it abides in the vine, so neither can you unless you abide in Me. I am the vine, you are the branches; he who abides in Me and I in him, he bears much fruit, for apart from Me you can do nothing. If anyone does not abide in Me, he is thrown away as a branch and dries up; and they gather them, and cast them into the fire and they are burned. (John 15:4–6)

Two things stand out in the passage that the modern reader has often been taught to suppress. The first is the conditional. *Unless you abide in Me*, the branch cannot bear fruit. The fruit-bearing is conditioned on the abiding. The second is the consequence. *If anyone does not abide in Me, he is thrown away as a branch and dries up*; the bundle is gathered and burned. This is not an abstract warning to people who never were in the vine to begin with. The verse is addressed to disciples — *you are the branches* — and the failure named is the failure of a branch in the vine to abide.

This passage alone, taken at the level of its surface words, ends any claim that a person once placed in Christ cannot fall away. The branch in the vine that does not abide is thrown away. There is no other reading available to a man who has not arrived at the verse with a system that requires another reading.

How does a branch abide? Jesus answers in the next verses. He does not leave it mystical or vague.

> If you abide in Me, and My words abide in you, ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you. (John 15:7)

> Just as the Father has loved Me, I have also loved you; abide in My love. If you keep My commandments, you will abide in My love; just as I have kept My Father's commandments and abide in His love. (John 15:9–10)

Three statements, all conditional, all together giving the picture. To abide in Christ is to have His words abiding in us (v. 7). To abide in Christ is to abide in His love (v. 9). To abide in His love is to keep His commandments (v. 10). Stitch the three together and the picture is plain. Abiding in Christ is keeping His commandments with His words dwelling in us. There is nothing mystical about it. It is a concrete and ongoing matter of holding His word in us and acting on it. The Christian who drifts from Christ's words — who stops reading, stops obeying, stops submitting — is the branch not abiding. And the branch not abiding does not stay neutral. It withers.

Verse 10 adds a striking note. Jesus tells His disciples to abide in His love by keeping His commandments — *just as I have kept My Father's commandments and abide in His love.* The pattern of commandment-keeping by Christ Himself is held up as the pattern for the disciple. Matthew 3 shows Jesus coming to John to be baptized. John objected — Jesus did not need cleansing. Jesus answered, "Permit it at this time; for in this way it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness" (Matt. 3:15). He obeyed because the Father had commanded. Hebrews 4:15 adds the range: He was "tempted in all things as we are, yet without sin." Philippians 2:8 adds the depth: "He humbled Himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross." Jesus kept the Father's commandments completely, unexceptionally, under every pressure, to the point of His own death. He then, in John 15:10, holds out *His own pattern of commandment-keeping* as the pattern of how His disciples are to abide in His love.

This is not a suggestion for the spiritually advanced. It is the structure of Christian life as Christ Himself defined it on the last night of His ministry, with the cross hours away.

## Romans 2: God Renders to Each According to His Deeds

Of all the New Testament texts that resist accommodation by faith-only schemes, Romans 2:6–10 stands at the head of the list. The book of Romans is the same book that argues for justification by faith apart from works of the Law (3:28). It is the same book that proves Abraham was justified by faith before he was circumcised (chapter 4). It is the book that says we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ (5:1) and that nothing can separate us from His love (8:39).

In the second chapter of that same book, Paul writes this:

> God will render to each person according to his deeds: to those who by perseverance in doing good seek for glory and honor and immortality, eternal life; but to those who are selfishly ambitious and do not obey the truth, but obey unrighteousness, wrath and indignation. There will be tribulation and distress for every soul of man who does evil, of the Jew first and also of the Greek, but glory and honor and peace to everyone who does good, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. (Rom. 2:6–10)

Read these verses without the system. The same God who, in Romans 3, accepts a man on the ground of faith in the propitiation of Christ rather than on the ground of works of the Law of Moses, says here in Romans 2 that *He will render to each person according to his deeds.* Eternal life goes to those who, by perseverance in doing good, seek for glory and honor and immortality. Wrath goes to those who do not obey the truth but obey unrighteousness. The criterion is named twice over: doing good versus doing evil. The two paths are described in the language of action.

There is no way to extract this passage from the apostle's own thinking. He wrote it. He wrote 3:28 also, and they sit a few pages apart from each other in the same letter. The man who reads Romans 3 against Romans 2 is reading Paul against Paul. The man who reads them together sees that justification by faith and judgment according to works are not contradictories in Paul's mind, because the faith that justifies is the faith that obeys, and the works that the judgment looks for are the works that flow from the obedience of faith Paul names in 1:5 and again in 16:26.

Two phrases from the passage deserve sustained attention.

First, *perseverance in doing good*. The word translated "perseverance" means active continuation under pressure, holding out when pressure is on. It is not passive. It does not mean simply continuing to exist. It means continuing to act in the right direction when other directions would be easier. Paul ties eternal life — eternal life — to perseverance in doing good. He uses the present tense. He is describing not a single past decision but an ongoing posture of life. If perseverance is the condition of eternal life, then a person who does not persevere does not receive it. That is simple logic on Paul's own sentence. The system that teaches that no one truly saved can ultimately fall away has read this passage and either bracketed it or supplied content that the apostle did not supply. He did not write that perseverance is the inevitable evidence of an unconditional election. He wrote that God renders to each according to his deeds and that those who persevere in doing good receive eternal life. He wrote what he wrote.

Other apostles say the same thing. Revelation 2:10: "Be faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life." Hebrews 3:14: "we have become partakers of Christ, *if* we hold fast the beginning of our assurance firm until the end." That *if* is not decorative. If holding fast until the end is required to remain a partaker, then failure to hold fast ends the partaking. Even Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ, wrote: "I discipline my body and make it my slave, so that, after I have preached to others, I myself will not be disqualified" (1 Cor. 9:27). If Paul could be disqualified, no one is guaranteed eternal life regardless of how he lives. Perseverance is not a gold-tier feature for the unusually devoted. It is the condition of the promise.

Second, *obey unrighteousness*. Paul does not write what we might expect. We expect to read of those who *yield to* unrighteousness or *give in to* unrighteousness, with a softer verb to match a less serious failure. Paul writes *obey*. The word he chooses for the wrong path is the same word he uses for the right one. He explains why a few chapters later, in Romans 6:16: "Do you not know that when you present yourselves to someone as slaves for obedience, you are slaves of the one whom you obey, either of sin resulting in death, or of obedience resulting in righteousness?" Sin is a master. The man who refuses to obey the truth is not standing in some neutral middle ground. He is serving a different master. Everyone obeys something. The question is what, or whom.

Romans 2:6–10 is the New Testament's most direct restatement of the Old Testament principle: God is impartial, He sees the works of every man, and He judges according to those works. Acts 10:34–35 said the same thing to Cornelius: "in every nation the man who fears Him and does what is right is welcome to Him." 1 Peter 1:17 said it to Christians: "If you address as Father the One who impartially judges according to each one's work, conduct yourselves in fear during the time of your stay on earth." The God who renders to each according to his work is the same God whose impartiality cleared away unconditional election in Chapter 1 of this book. He is consistent. He is holy. He is unchanged.

## Colossians 1: The Conditional Continuance

A few letters later in Paul's collection, the conditional structure surfaces again — this time in a verse most often quoted with its conditional clipped off.

> And although you were formerly alienated and hostile in mind, engaged in evil deeds, yet He has now reconciled you in His fleshly body through death, in order to present you before Him holy and blameless and beyond reproach — if indeed you continue in the faith firmly established and steadfast, and not moved away from the hope of the gospel that you have heard. (Col. 1:21–23)

The first half of the sentence is what is commonly quoted: *He has now reconciled you in His fleshly body through death, in order to present you before Him holy and blameless and beyond reproach.* That is a glorious sentence. Christ has reconciled the formerly alienated. The presentation before God will be holy, blameless, beyond reproach. So far, so quotable.

Then comes the *if*. Paul does not let the sentence land without qualifying it. The presentation will happen *if indeed you continue in the faith firmly established and steadfast, and not moved away from the hope of the gospel that you have heard.* Four descriptions of the continuing: *continue, firmly established, steadfast, not moved away.* The verb is in the present tense. It describes ongoing posture, not a past commitment. And the condition gates the promise. If the Christians at Colossae did not continue — if they drifted from the hope of the gospel — Paul's sentence does not promise them presentation before God as holy and blameless. The *if* is real, and what it guards is real.

This verse is one of the cleanest rebuttals in Scripture to the doctrine that says a true Christian cannot fall away. Paul was writing to Christians, reconciled through Christ's death, and he still hung the final outcome on the condition of continuing. A system that says the *if* cannot actually fail is a system that has taken Paul's word and drained the condition out of it. If the *if* in Colossians 1:23 cannot fail, Paul wasted four phrases describing what continuance looks like. He did not waste them. He wrote them because the failure is possible, the warning is needed, and the steadfast continuance is the condition of the promised presentation.

## 1 Corinthians 15: The Apostle's Own If-Then Chain

Sometimes a New Testament writer reasons in conditional structure so consciously that the structure itself becomes the argument. Paul does this in 1 Corinthians 15, and it is worth pausing over.

Some at Corinth had been saying there is no resurrection of the dead (v. 12). Paul's response is not to assert the resurrection again. It is to follow the chain of *ifs* that flow from denying it, and to show the Corinthians that the position they think is small actually collapses everything. Verses 13–19 contain a striking series of conditional statements. Read them together:

> But if there is no resurrection of the dead, not even Christ has been raised; and if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is vain, your faith also is vain. Moreover we are even found to be false witnesses of God, because we testified against God that He raised Christ, whom He did not raise, if in fact the dead are not raised. For if the dead are not raised, not even Christ has been raised; and if Christ has not been raised, your faith is worthless; you are still in your sins. Then those also who have fallen asleep in Christ have perished. If we have hoped in Christ in this life only, we are of all men most to be pitied. (1 Cor. 15:13–19)

Seven *ifs*. Each one drawing out a consequence of denying the resurrection. The chain is rigorous. *If* there is no resurrection of the dead, then Christ has not been raised; *if* Christ has not been raised, then preaching is vain; *if* preaching is vain, then faith is also vain; *if* faith is vain, then those still in sin remain in sin; *if* those who have died in Christ are not raised, they have perished; *if* hope in Christ is for this life only, the Christian is most to be pitied.

Then verse 20: *But now Christ has been raised from the dead, the first fruits of those who are asleep.* That single sentence reverses every if-then in the chain. The dead are raised; therefore preaching is not vain; therefore faith is not vain; therefore those in Christ are no longer in their sins; therefore those who have fallen asleep have not perished; therefore Christian hope is not for this life only.

Paul reasons this way for a reason. He is teaching the Corinthians how to think about the gospel. He is showing them that the conditional structure of theological reasoning is the gospel's structure, that *if-then* is not a foreign category brought in to embarrass faith but the apostle's own way of pressing the consequences of belief on the believer's conscience. If the same apostle reasons in *ifs* about Christ's resurrection, the same apostle's *ifs* about Christian continuance and obedience are not casual. They are the apostle's way of showing how the gospel actually structures life.

## Hebrews 10:36 — The Promise Past the Endurance

The writer of Hebrews wrote his letter to Christians who were considering walking away from Christ to return to the Mosaic system from which they had come. Through the letter he warns them, instructs them, and presses them to continue. In the back half of chapter 10, after a sustained warning that is one of the New Testament's most severe (10:26–31, a passage that will receive its full treatment in the next chapter), he says this:

> For you have need of endurance, so that when you have done the will of God, you may receive what was promised. (Heb. 10:36)

Read the structure of this sentence. The promise is named: *what was promised*. The point at which the promise is received is named: *when you have done the will of God*. The way to get from here to there is named: *endurance*. The promise is not received this side of the doing. It is received on the far side, when the doing has been accomplished. *When you have done the will of God, you may receive what was promised.*

The word translated "endurance" here is the same word translated "perseverance" in Romans 2:7. It is the same idea. Steadfast continuance under pressure, patient bearing up, holding out when it would be easier to stop. Active continuation, not passive existence.

The relation to doing God's will is straightforward. Obedience is easy when nothing is pushing against it. When persecution comes — and the verses just before, Heb. 10:32–34, describe exactly that, with the readers having lost property and been publicly exposed for the name of Christ — when friends fall away, when obedience becomes costly, that is when endurance is required. The writer puts the promise on the far side of the endurance, not this side of it. No endurance, no promise received. That is the plain structure of the sentence.

This passage prepares the reader for the conditional warnings that occupy the next chapter of this book. Hebrews is the book where the New Testament's most severe warnings about falling away appear, and they appear because the author of Hebrews believes — as Paul believes, as John believes, as the Lord Himself believes — that the conditional structure of God's relationship to His people is real. The Hebrews 6 warning, the Hebrews 10 warning, the parable of the branches, the imperative of 2 Peter 1:10 to make one's calling and choosing sure: all of these texts presume that the conditions are conditions, that they can fail, and that the failure has consequences.

The next chapter takes those warnings up. This chapter is content to note that the writer of Hebrews has already shown his hand. The promise is received past the endurance. There is no shortcut.

## John on Walking and Knowing

John, in his first epistle, presses the conditional structure into the inner life. He is writing to Christians, and he is asking them — calmly, repeatedly, in conditional terms — whether their lives are actually what they claim they are.

Consider 1 John 1:6–7:

> If we say that we have fellowship with Him and yet walk in the darkness, we lie and do not practice the truth; but if we walk in the Light as He Himself is in the Light, we have fellowship with one another, and the blood of Jesus His Son cleanses us from all sin.

Two conditions, two responses, set side by side. *If we say…and yet walk in the darkness*, we lie. *If we walk in the Light*, we have fellowship and the blood cleanses. The verb *walk* is the verb of ongoing manner of life. Paul uses the same image in Ephesians 2:2 of the unbelievers' former life: "in which you formerly walked according to the course of this world." A walk is not a stumble. It is a direction.

To walk in darkness is to live in the realm opposite to God's nature. John has just said in v. 5 that "God is Light, and in Him there is no darkness at all." To walk in darkness is to live in willful evasion of God's truth, with one's deeds hidden from His light because they cannot stand it. John 3:19–21 describes the same posture: "men loved the darkness rather than the Light, for their deeds were evil." It is not a single failure; it is a pattern.

To walk in the Light is to live in harmony with God's revealed nature and will. This is not sinlessness. John in the very next breath (v. 8) tells those who claim to have no sin that they are deceived, and (v. 9) names confession of sins as the practice of the walker in light. Walking in the light is the honest, ongoing pursuit of God's truth and righteousness, with sin brought into the open and dealt with rather than hidden. Two results follow: fellowship with one another, and continual cleansing by the blood of Jesus. The verb "cleanses" is in the present tense — ongoing. Both results are tied to the condition. The blood does not cleanse the one walking in darkness. It cleanses the one walking in the light.

A chapter later, John drives the same point even harder.

> By this we know that we have come to know Him, if we keep His commandments. The one who says, "I have come to know Him," and does not keep His commandments, is a liar, and the truth is not in him; but whoever keeps His word, in him the love of God has truly been perfected. By this we know that we are in Him: the one who says he abides in Him ought himself to walk in the same manner as He walked. (1 John 2:3–6)

*If we keep His commandments*, we know that we have come to know Him. The keeping is the test. Without the keeping, the claim to know Him is *a lie*. John uses the word *liar* for the man who claims to know Christ and does not keep His commandments. This is direct, even harsh, and the apostle of love is the one writing it.

The "knowing" John has in view is not bare information. John 17:3 — "this is eternal life, that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent." That knowing *is* eternal life, which means it involves reconciled relationship, not bare facts. But the test John gives is behavioral. Obedience is not only the fruit of knowing God; it is the evidence by which we know that we know Him. Feelings, professions, religious experiences, intensity of conviction — none of these is the measure. Commandment-keeping is. If a man's life is not marked by obedience, his claim to know God is false regardless of what he says or feels.

Verse 5 adds the positive side. *Whoever keeps His word, in him the love of God has truly been perfected.* The word translated "perfected" means brought to its intended completion, fully realized, reaching the purpose for which it was given. God's love has an intended outcome in the man who receives it — that the man walk in the same manner as Christ walked (v. 6). When we keep His word, that outcome is reaching completion in us. The love we received from God was not a feeling we worked up; it was His love given to us, now working its way into our lived obedience, which is the shape God meant it to take.

To round out John's witness, consider one more verse — a sentence from Jesus's own lips on the night of the Last Supper.

> If you know these things, you are blessed if you do them. (John 13:17)

Two conditions. *If you know these things* — the head must have the truth. *If you do them* — the life must put it into practice. Both required. The blessing rests on both.

The implied opposite is stark. You are *not* blessed if you know these things and do not do them. James 4:17 makes the inference explicit: "to one who knows the right thing to do and does not do it, to him it is sin." Luke 12:47 warns that "that slave who knew his master's will and did not get ready or act in accord with his will, will receive many lashes." Knowledge without doing is not neutral. Information increases accountability, not safety. The man who fills his head with Bible knowledge while remaining outside obedience does not insulate himself from judgment by his learning. He compounds his exposure to it.

## The "Faith Only" Question

We have walked through fifteen New Testament passages. They span the four Gospels, eight epistles, and the writer of Hebrews. They speak with the voices of Jesus, Paul, John, and the unnamed hand of Hebrews. Every one of them states a condition for the promise it offers, and the condition is some form of obedient response — keeping commandments, doing the will of the Father, denying self, taking up the cross, abiding in the vine, walking in the light, persevering in doing good, continuing in the faith, doing what one knows.

These passages now press the obvious question. If the New Testament's structure is so plainly conditional, where does the doctrine come from that says salvation is by faith only?

The doctrine has its modern shape in the work of Martin Luther. In his 1522 German New Testament, Luther translated Romans 3:28 — Paul's "a man is justified by faith apart from works of the Law" — by inserting the word *alone*: "a man is justified by faith alone, apart from works of the Law." Luther defended the addition openly. He acknowledged that the word "alone" was not in the original text but argued that the German language required it to make the meaning vigorous.[^1] Whatever one thinks of his decision as a translator, the simple historical fact is that Luther's "alone" was not in the apostle's text. It was a translation choice, made by a man, in service of a doctrine he was developing.

The Reformers of the sixteenth century, having inherited centuries of Roman Catholic teaching that bound salvation to priestly ceremonies, the purchase of indulgences, and works the church could prescribe to make satisfaction for sin, swung hard in the opposite direction. The corrective they offered was real: salvation is not by works of merit, not by the priest's pronouncement, not by purchased indulgence, not by ceremonies performed by men in vestments. So far the Reformers were right, and their reading of Paul against the Roman system was healthy. But the corrective they put forward — salvation by faith alone — was itself a reaction, and reactions tend to overshoot.

The slogan overshoots in this way. The New Testament does indeed teach justification by faith. Paul says so plainly. But the New Testament does not teach justification by faith *alone*, in the sense in which the slogan is used to mean faith without obedience. There is, in fact, exactly one verse in all of Scripture that uses the phrase "faith alone." It is James 2:24, and it is preceded by the word *not*.

> You see that a man is justified by works and not by faith alone. (Jas. 2:24)

This is not a verse to be hurried past or explained away. It is the only verse in the New Testament that contains the precise phrase the Reformers turned into a slogan. And the verse, in the only place where the phrase occurs, says the opposite of the slogan. *Not by faith alone.*

James writes this in the middle of a chapter (2:14–26) whose entire concern is the relation between faith and works. He opens the section with a question: "What use is it, my brethren, if someone says he has faith but he has no works? Can that faith save him?" (v. 14). The grammatical form of the Greek expects the answer *no*. He gives a vivid illustration of empty profession: telling a brother in need to be warm and well-fed without giving him anything to eat or wear (vv. 15–16). He draws the conclusion: "even so faith, if it has no works, is dead, being by itself" (v. 17). He addresses an objector who tries to separate the two — *you have faith and I have works* — and answers: "show me your faith without the works, and I will show you my faith by my works" (v. 18). He observes that even the demons believe, and shudder (v. 19). The "faith" of empty profession is not the saving faith of the New Testament. It is a faith the demons share. And then comes the verse:

> You see that a man is justified by works and not by faith alone. (Jas. 2:24)

Every major English translation, from the King James Version through the NASB, renders James 2:24 so that "faith alone" or "faith only" stands together as the thing James is denying. The translators have not made a mistake. They are reflecting the apostle's argument.

It is sometimes objected that James and Paul are using the word *justified* in different senses — Paul to mean "declared righteous in God's sight" and James to mean "demonstrated to be righteous before men." There is something to this distinction. Paul is concerned in Romans and Galatians with how a sinner stands accepted before a holy God; James is concerned with the lived evidence of saving faith. But the distinction does not save the slogan. James himself uses the same illustration Paul uses in Romans 4 — Abraham's faith, reckoned to him as righteousness — and James draws the conclusion that Abraham's faith was brought to completion when he obeyed in Genesis 22 by offering Isaac. Faith, in James, is not the absence of works. It is the principle that, when alive, *produces* works, and when not producing works, is dead and saves no one.

James is consistent with Paul. Paul nowhere teaches that justifying faith is a faith disconnected from obedience. Paul calls his apostolic mission "to bring about the obedience of faith among all the Gentiles" (Rom. 1:5; 16:26). He warns the same Galatians who heard him preach justification by faith that "those who practice such things will not inherit the kingdom of God" (Gal. 5:21). He writes to the Christians at Corinth that "neither fornicators…will inherit the kingdom of God" (1 Cor. 6:9–10). He tells the Romans, "if you are living according to the flesh, you must die; but if by the Spirit you are putting to death the deeds of the body, you will live" (Rom. 8:13). Paul does not contradict James. He does not teach that the faith which justifies is a faith that need not produce obedience. He teaches what James teaches: a living faith works, and a dead faith — a faith without works — saves no one.

When the doctrine of faith alone presses James 2:24 as somehow not quite contradicting it, the doctrine is asking the New Testament to say something its only verse with the phrase "faith alone" denies. The slogan stands on translation history and the systematizing of later teachers. The verse stands where the apostle put it.

## "Believe and Be Saved" Texts in Their Actual Contexts

Three verses are most often cited in defense of the slogan that salvation is by faith alone. Each, when read in its actual context, says less than the slogan claims and more than the slogan can accommodate.

### Acts 16:31

The Philippian jailer, having been on the verge of suicide and just been spared by Paul's voice, falls trembling before Paul and Silas and asks: "Sirs, what must I do to be saved?" (Acts 16:30). The apostles answer:

> Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household. (Acts 16:31)

Read in isolation, that looks like the slogan in five words. Believe and be saved. End of conversation.

But the conversation does not end at v. 31. The next verse says, "And they spoke the word of the Lord to him together with all who were in his house" (v. 32). The apostles taught the jailer the gospel — they did not stop at "believe." Verse 33: "And he took them that very hour of the night and washed their wounds, and immediately he was baptized, he and all his household." The same hour of the night, the jailer washed the apostles' wounds (a costly act — he was the one who had recently been in charge of inflicting their suffering) and then *was baptized*, he and his entire household. Only after that — verse 34 — does Luke describe the jailer as "having believed in God." Belief was completed in baptism, that very hour, with the entire family. The "believe" of v. 31 is filled in by what the next three verses report the man actually doing.

A reader who claims that Acts 16:31 teaches faith alone — faith without baptism, faith without obedient response — is taking v. 31 out of the four verses of which it is one part. He cannot do this without breaking the text. The same Luke who wrote v. 31 wrote vv. 32–34. The same household that believed was baptized, that very hour. The "believe" of v. 31 is the believe that was answered by teaching, and then by baptism, and then by joy.

### Romans 10:9

The second commonly cited verse is Romans 10:9.

> If you confess with your mouth Jesus as Lord, and believe in your heart that God raised Him from the dead, you will be saved. (Rom. 10:9)

This verse is sometimes pressed as the formula for salvation: confess Jesus as Lord, believe He was raised, and that is the whole transaction. But Paul's whole letter is the context, and Paul's whole letter is what the verse stands inside.

Paul has just said in 10:3 that the Jews of Israel were "not subjecting themselves to the righteousness of God" because they were "seeking to establish their own" through the works of the Law. That is the contrast Paul is drawing in chapter 10 — Mosaic Law-righteousness versus the righteousness that comes by faith in the risen Christ. He is *not* drawing a contrast between confessing-and-believing on the one side and obeying Christ on the other.

Paul could not be drawing such a contrast, because four chapters earlier in the same letter he had written this:

> Or do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus have been baptized into His death? Therefore we have been buried with Him through baptism into death, so that as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we too might walk in newness of life. (Rom. 6:3–4)

The same Paul who wrote 10:9 wrote 6:3–4. The "believe in your heart that God raised Him from the dead" of 10:9 is the same belief that, in 6:3–4, places the believer into Christ's death by baptism. Paul does not give two different gospels. He gives one gospel from two different angles.

And six verses after 10:9, Paul writes: "However, they did not all heed the good news; for Isaiah says, 'LORD, who has believed our report?'" (Rom. 10:16). The verb "heed" in v. 16 is the verb of obedience. Heeding the gospel is the same thing as obeying it. Not all did. The salvation Paul describes in 10:9 is the salvation of those who heed the good news. To heed is to obey.

Romans 10:9 is therefore a true statement, but not the slogan it has been turned into. It is one angle on the response that brings a man into Christ — confession and belief — set against the alternative of trying to establish one's own righteousness by Law-works. It is not a list of all that is required, written in opposition to the rest of what Paul has just said about being baptized into Christ's death. The rest of Paul stands; 10:9 stands inside it.

### John 3:16

The third commonly cited verse is the most famous in the New Testament:

> For God so loved the world, that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him shall not perish, but have eternal life. (John 3:16)

Read by itself, with no context, this verse can be made to sound like the slogan with God's own signature on it. *Whoever believes in Him shall not perish.* Believe and live. The whole gospel in one sentence. So the slogan goes.

But John 3:16 sits inside a conversation Jesus is having with Nicodemus, and the conversation began thirteen verses earlier with this:

> Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born of water and the Spirit he cannot enter into the kingdom of God. (John 3:5)

Whatever John 3:16 means, it cannot mean a salvation that takes place apart from being born of water and the Spirit, because the very Lord who said v. 16 said v. 5 to the same man, eleven verses earlier, in the same conversation. To take 3:16 as a faith-only statement disconnected from 3:5 is to take Jesus against Jesus.

Furthermore, the verb *believe* in John's Gospel is not bare mental agreement with the proposition that Jesus is the Christ. It is sustained reliance on Him as Lord, which produces obedience. John's own conclusion to his Gospel says exactly this. "Therefore many other signs Jesus also performed in the presence of the disciples, which are not written in this book; but these have been written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing you may have life in His name" (John 20:30–31). The believing John is summoning his readers to is the believing that issues in life — life lived in submission to the Christ confessed.

And John 3:36, twenty verses after 3:16, says this:

> He who believes in the Son has eternal life; but he who does not obey the Son will not see life, but the wrath of God abides on him. (John 3:36)

In the second clause of this sentence, John's contrast to *believes in the Son* is not *does not believe in the Son* — it is *does not obey the Son*. The two are equated. To believe in the Son is to obey the Son. To refuse to obey the Son is to refuse to believe in Him. John makes the equation at the end of the same chapter that contains John 3:16. The believing of John 3:16 is the obeying of John 3:36. They are the same thing.

If a man wishes to teach faith only in the sense that bare mental belief, apart from obedient response to the Lord, brings eternal life, he cannot get there from John 3:16 without traveling against John 3:5 and John 3:36. He cannot get there from Romans 10:9 without traveling against Romans 6:3–4 and Romans 10:16. He cannot get there from Acts 16:31 without traveling against Acts 16:32–34. The three texts most commonly cited for the slogan, read in their own contexts, do not yield the slogan.

What they yield instead is what the rest of this chapter has shown: a New Testament whose conditional structure is unbroken, whose author is the same God who set conditions in Eden and at Sinai and at Moab and through the prophets, and whose gospel calls men to a faith that obeys, an obedience that loves, and a love that keeps His commandments — because the Lord whose name they confess is the same yesterday and today and forever.

## Looking Forward

This chapter has walked through more than fifteen New Testament passages. The pattern is uniform and the conclusion is unavoidable. From the Sermon on the Mount through the upper room discourse, from Paul's first letter to his last, from Hebrews to James to the epistles of John, the structure of God's relation to His people in the New Covenant is the same structure as in the Old: stated conditions, named promises, real warnings, real consequences, called for from real moral creatures whose response is real.

The Lord who said *if you wish to enter into life, keep the commandments* is the Lord who said *I am the way, and the truth, and the life; no one comes to the Father but through Me.* He is one Lord, and He speaks the same way in both sentences. The Father who weeps over Jerusalem because she would not come is the same Father who renders to each according to his deeds. The God of the New Testament is the God of the Old. He has not changed.

What this chapter has prepared the way for, the next chapter takes up. The conditions named carry warnings with them. *If you forget the LORD your God, you will surely perish.* *Unless you abide in Me, you cannot bear fruit.* *If you sin willfully after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins.* The warnings are conditional. The warnings are real. And the warnings are addressed to people the systems of modern theology have insisted cannot, in fact, ever fall away.

The next chapter walks through those warnings, in the texts where the New Testament places them, with the consequences the New Testament names. The conditional structure is the same. What changes is the weight. When the conditions concern not entry but continuance — not whether a man comes to Christ but whether he stays in Him — the warnings press on places the modern conscience has been carefully insulated from feeling. The next chapter walks into that ground.

For now, the close of this chapter is simply this: the God of Sinai is the God of Calvary. The covenant has changed. The conditioner of the covenant has not. *He has not changed.* And the man who has read this chapter has now seen, in the words of Christ Himself, the texts that show it.
