*Preface*

# A Word Before We Begin

This book exists because of another book.

In 2026, Charles Willis published *God's Conditions* — a workbook of nine lessons that walks the student through the Scriptures God has used to state His terms. The terms for entering Christ. The terms for remaining in Him. The terms for prayer. The terms for warning. The terms for life. Lesson by lesson, the workbook gathers passages that the typical reader has heard quoted in fragments but rarely seen assembled, and asks the student to do something most religious literature does not ask him to do: read the texts, underline the conditions, and answer the questions himself.

The workbook is excellent, and it does what a workbook is supposed to do. It puts the reader in the texts. It treats him as capable of seeing what is on the page once the page is opened. It does not lecture him; it lets the apostles lecture him. By the time a student finishes Lesson 9, he has handled the relevant Scriptures himself and can say, on his own authority, that the structure of the gospel is conditional — that God expects obedience, that those who love Him obey, and that this is the same expectation in both Testaments because God Himself does not change.

But a workbook, by its nature, can do only so much. It cannot pause to engage the systems that have grown up around the texts and obscured them — Calvinism, Arminianism, dispensationalism, eternal security, faith-only, the various denominational traditions that read Scripture through their own lenses. It cannot trace the historical lineage of the doctrines it contests. It cannot give sustained prose treatment to the warning passages that most popular doctrines cannot accommodate. It cannot address the reader who, at the end of the study, finds himself standing in a position different from where he stood at the beginning. These are tasks for a book.

This is that book.

## Why the Order Matters

It is unusual to write a book *after* a workbook. The normal order is the other way: a writer publishes his book first, and a study guide follows for those who want to work through the material more deeply. We have taken the inverted approach because the workbook deserves it.

If a book had been written first and the workbook had followed, the workbook would inevitably read as a vehicle for the book's argument. Every question would be a question whose answer the reader had already been given. The student would be doing review, not discovery. The conviction he ends with would be the writer's conviction received, not his own conviction built.

Charles wrote the workbook first, which means the student who works through it builds his case from the texts themselves. Only after that work is done does this book step in to do what the workbook left undone — to face the systems by name, to handle the proof-texts the systems lean on, and to bring the argument to a landing.

The two are designed to be used together. Either order works. A reader can work through Lesson 1 in the workbook and then read Chapter 1 here, taking the lessons and the chapters in parallel. Or he can read this book straight through and use the workbook afterward to verify each claim from Scripture himself. The second order will satisfy the reader who likes to see the argument first; the first order will satisfy the reader who likes to build his own conviction before reading anyone else's. Both orders end at the same place.

## The Discipline of This Book

A book about conditions in Scripture must hold itself to conditions of its own. Three are non-negotiable in what follows.

**Scripture is the sole authority.** When Scripture and human teaching disagree — denominational confession, historical commentary, popular tradition, the consensus of scholarship, anything else — Scripture wins. This is not a slogan; it is a working rule, and the reader will see it operating on every page. When the Westminster Confession says one thing and Acts 10:34–35 says another, the chapter does not look for a way to make them agree. It states what Westminster says, states what Acts says, and lets the reader see that they cannot both be true.

**The post-apostolic vocabulary that obscures Scripture is set aside.** A great many of the phrases that pass for Christian teaching today are not Scripture's language. *Sinner's prayer.* *Eternal security.* *Once saved, always saved.* *Asking Jesus into your heart.* *Sprinkling.* *Sacrament.* *Rapture.* These phrases are not in the New Testament, and several of them describe practices and doctrines the New Testament does not teach. Where the book engages those phrases, it engages them as the products of the systems they belong to, not as terms to be assumed. Where Scripture has its own words, this book uses Scripture's words.

**Sources are verified, and quotations are accurate.** The book quotes Calvin's *Institutes*, the Westminster Confession, the Canons of Dort, and similar historical documents at various points. Every such quotation has been checked against the actual document, not against memory or summary. Where a translation issue exists — as with Calvin's *decretum horribile* — the issue is named in a footnote so the reader can see the question rather than have it hidden. The reader who wants to verify any historical claim made in this book will find footnotes pointing him to the sources he needs.

These three commitments are simply the application, to the writing of a book, of the same commitments the workbook has already required of the student.

## What the Reader Will Encounter

The book follows the workbook's lesson order, with a chapter for each lesson and a closing chapter at the end. Each chapter takes up the same texts the corresponding lesson takes up, but treats them in sustained prose rather than question form, names the systems and traditions the workbook touches more lightly, and gives the warning passages and the conditional structures the room they need.

Some chapters will challenge the reader. The chapter on election asks the reader who has been taught Calvinist or Reformed doctrine to consider that the system he has received may not actually be in his Bible. The chapter on falling away asks the reader who has been taught that he cannot lose his salvation to face the texts that say otherwise — and there are a great many of them. The chapter on salvation asks the reader who has been taught that one need only "ask Jesus into his heart" to face Acts 2:38, Acts 22:16, Romans 6:3–4, and Galatians 3:27 in their plain reading. None of this is meant to provoke. It is meant to put the reader in front of texts he may not have seen handled this way before, and to ask him to make his own judgment about what they say.

The book is not denominational polemic. It does not exist to defend any church or any movement against any other. It exists to put a reader in front of God's word and let him see what God's word actually says about the conditions God has named. If at any point the book argues for a position that cannot be supported from Scripture, the book is wrong and Scripture is right. That is the only standard the book will own.

## Who This Book Is For

This book is for the Christian who suspects that something has been left out of the gospel he was taught. It is for the seeker who has heard six different answers to "How am I saved?" from six different people who all claim to use the same Bible. It is for the longtime believer who wants to see the texts behind the slogans. It is for the careful student who, like Charles's workbook student, would rather see the case built from the passages than be told the conclusion and asked to trust the writer.

It is not for the reader who wants confirmation of what he already believes without being asked to look at any text that might disturb that belief. Such a reader will find the book uncomfortable. That is by design. Scripture is uncomfortable when it tells us something different from what we wish were true. The discomfort is the beginning of repentance, and repentance is one of the conditions God has stated.

## Now We Begin

The chapter that follows takes up Lesson 1 of the workbook — the human teachings about election that have crowded out what Scripture actually says. From there the book proceeds lesson by lesson, chapter by chapter, until at the end we ask the question every honest reader has to ask himself by then.

Jesus said, "If you love Me, you will keep My commandments" (John 14:15). The conditional structure of that sentence is the structure of this entire book. The promise is real, and the condition is real, and the reader who finishes the book is the one who has to decide what he will do with both.

May God grant you the honest heart of a Berean, the humble discipline of a learner, and the courage of one who would rather find out he was wrong than continue believing what is false. There is no other way to read His word well.
