*Chapter 1*

# The Teachings of Man About Election

There is a question buried underneath every page of this book. Did God decide before the foundation of the world who would be saved, without regard for any response from those persons, so that the saved are saved unconditionally and the lost are lost unconditionally? Or did He set the terms on which men may be saved, offer them genuinely to all, and let each man answer for himself?

The two positions cannot both be true. They are not nuances of the same doctrine; they are opposite doctrines. One of them describes a God who shows no partiality, who tells His messengers to preach to every creature, who pleads with sinners to repent, who weeps over a city that will not come to Him. The other describes a God who arranged the eternal destiny of every man before that man existed and whose preaching, pleading, and weeping must therefore be a kind of public theater addressed only to those whose destiny was already settled in His favor. The first is the God of Scripture. The second is the God of the system — and it is the system, with its texts and its history and its terrible weight on the conscience of the church, that this opening chapter is about.

## Where the System Came From

The version of election that this book contests is usually called *unconditional election*. It is the second of the five points of what is now known as Calvinism, summarized by the acronym TULIP: total depravity, unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace, perseverance of the saints. The acronym was coined in the early twentieth century, but the doctrine it summarizes is older.[^1]

Augustine of Hippo (354–430) was the major Christian writer who first developed the position that God's election of individuals to salvation rests entirely on God's own choice apart from any foreseen response in those individuals.[^2] He developed this position late in life, in his controversy with the British monk Pelagius. Pelagius emphasized human moral capacity in ways Augustine took to deny the necessity of divine grace, and Augustine was right to insist that grace is necessary. But in opposing Pelagius, he swung past Scripture and arrived at a doctrine the apostles never taught: that grace is not offered to all but bestowed irresistibly on those whom God has chosen, while the rest are left in their lost condition by His sovereign decree.

A thousand years later, John Calvin (1509–1564) systematized Augustine's position and gave it the form it carries today. In his *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, first published in 1536 and revised through several editions until 1559, Calvin defined predestination as "God's eternal decree, by which He determined with Himself what He willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others."[^3] Calvin acknowledged the difficulty of his own teaching. He wrote of the predestination of the lost to damnation as a *decretum horribile* — a "dreadful decree" — but held it nonetheless because he believed Scripture required it.[^4]

After Calvin, the doctrine was codified by his theological successors. The Synod of Dort in the Netherlands (1618–1619) met to respond to the Arminian Remonstrance, which had challenged five points of Calvinist teaching. The Synod's response affirmed the five points that would later become TULIP.[^5] In 1646, the Westminster Assembly in England produced the Westminster Confession of Faith.[^6] Chapter III declares that "some men and angels are predestinated unto everlasting life, and others foreordained to everlasting death" (§3), and that the number of each is so certain that it "cannot be either increased or diminished" (§4). Section 5 then spells out the basis of the choice:

> Those of mankind that are predestinated unto life, God, before the foundation of the world was laid, according to His eternal and immutable purpose, and the secret counsel and good pleasure of His will, hath chosen, in Christ, unto everlasting glory, out of His mere free grace and love, without any foresight of faith, or good works, or perseverance in either of them, or any other thing in the creature, as conditions, or causes moving Him thereunto.

That same language, with minor adjustments, was carried into the Baptist Confession of Faith of 1689[^7] and into the Presbyterian confessions. It is what is taught today by Reformed seminaries, by the new Calvinist resurgence among young evangelicals, and by countless pulpits where the system is held even when it is not articulated by name.

This is the lineage of the doctrine. It comes to us through Augustine, then Calvin, then Dort, then Westminster, then the modern Reformed movement. It does not come to us through the apostles. The New Testament writers never quote any source for it because there is no source for it in the Old Testament. Paul, who used the word "predestined" in his letters, used it in ways that no Calvinist confession has ever quoted in full — because he used it differently than the system requires.

## What Scripture Actually Says About Predestination

The Greek word for "predestined" is *proorizō*, "to mark out beforehand." It appears six times in the New Testament. Six. Here are all of them.

Acts 4:28 — Peter and the church praying after Peter and John's release: "to do whatever Your hand and Your purpose predestined to occur." The thing predestined: the death of Christ at the hands of Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles, and the peoples of Israel.

Romans 8:29 — "For those whom He foreknew, He also predestined to become conformed to the image of His Son." The thing predestined: that those whom God foreknew would be conformed to the image of His Son.

Romans 8:30 — "and these whom He predestined, He also called; and these whom He called, He also justified; and these whom He justified, He also glorified." The thing predestined: a chain of saving acts that God brings to those previously named in v. 29.

1 Corinthians 2:7 — "but we speak God's wisdom in a mystery, the hidden wisdom which God predestined before the ages to our glory." The thing predestined: the wisdom of God in the gospel.

Ephesians 1:5 — "He predestined us to adoption as sons through Jesus Christ to Himself, according to the kind intention of His will." The thing predestined: adoption as sons through Jesus Christ.

Ephesians 1:11 — "we have obtained an inheritance, having been predestined according to His purpose." The thing predestined: the inheritance.

Six occurrences. In every case, the thing predestined is *what* — what would happen to those who would belong to Christ. In not one case is the thing predestined *who* — which individuals would be saved and which would not. The Calvinist doctrine of unconditional election asserts a *who* that the New Testament's actual use of the word "predestined" never asserts.

This is not a minor textual point. It is the textual point. The system has built a doctrine of who-is-saved on a word that Scripture uses for what-is-promised. Every Reformed confession quoted above asserts that God predestined specific individuals to life and others to death "before the foundation of the world." Scripture uses the word "predestined" of God's plan for those who are in Christ — the plan being conformity to His Son, adoption, glory, inheritance. The plan exists from before the foundation of the world. The individuals who enter into it do so on the terms God has stated, in time, by responding to the gospel.

## Ephesians 1:3–14 — the Chapter That Is Supposed to Prove Unconditional Election

Of all the texts in Scripture, Ephesians 1:3–14 is the one most often cited to prove unconditional election. It contains both occurrences of "predestined" listed above, it speaks of God's choice "before the foundation of the world" (v. 4), and it grounds salvation in "the kind intention of His will" (v. 5) and "the counsel of His will" (v. 11).

The passage is one long sentence in the Greek — literally one of the longest sentences in the New Testament — and that matters. To take a phrase out of it without reading the whole is to lift a stone out of an arch and ask what it is holding up. Read whole, the passage answers the very question Calvinism asks of it, but in a direction Calvinism does not go.

Verses 3–4: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ, just as He chose us in Him before the foundation of the world, that we would be holy and blameless before Him."

Notice the prepositional phrase: *in Him*. God chose us *in Him*. The choice of God before the foundation of the world is not the choice of certain individuals to be saved unconditionally; it is the choice of *those who would be in Christ* to be holy and blameless. The category was determined before the foundation of the world. The individuals fill the category by being placed into Christ in time.

This is the consistent New Testament pattern. Christ is the Chosen One (Luke 9:35; 1 Pet. 2:4). Those who are in Him share His chosen status. Those who are not in Him do not. Election in the New Testament is corporate before it is individual — it is election in Christ — and the way an individual becomes elect is by being placed into Christ. The question of how that placement happens is the subject of the rest of this book. The question is not closed by Ephesians 1:4. It is opened.

Verse 5: "He predestined us to adoption as sons through Jesus Christ to Himself, according to the kind intention of His will."

Predestined to *what*? To adoption as sons through Jesus Christ. That is what was predestined. Not the identity of the sons, but the destiny of those who would be sons — that they would be adopted, not enslaved; that they would come through Jesus Christ, not through any other route; that this adoption was always His "kind intention." The verse does not say He predestined which individuals would be sons. It says He predestined that those who became sons would be adopted ones, with full standing, through Christ.

Verses 11–13: "In Him also we have obtained an inheritance, having been predestined according to His purpose who works all things after the counsel of His will, to the end that we who were the first to hope in Christ would be to the praise of His glory. In Him, you also, after listening to the message of truth, the gospel of your salvation — having also believed, you were sealed in Him with the Holy Spirit of promise."

Here is the conditional structure that Calvinism cannot accommodate. Verse 13 tells us *how* the Ephesians came to be in Christ: *after listening to the message of truth, the gospel of your salvation — having also believed*. They listened. They believed. *Then* they were sealed in Him. The chain is hearing, then believing, then being sealed in Christ. Each link is a response. Paul does not say they were sealed in Christ from before the foundation of the world. He says they were sealed *after* they heard and believed.

The whole passage, read whole, says this: God determined from before the foundation of the world that those who would be in Christ would be holy, blameless, adopted, and sealed with the Spirit. He did not determine which individuals would be in Christ. He determined the destiny of the category. Individuals enter the category in time, by responding to the gospel they hear.

## Romans 8:28–30

The other major proof-text is Romans 8:28–30: "And we know that God causes all things to work together for good to those who love God, to those who are called according to His purpose. For those whom He foreknew, He also predestined to become conformed to the image of His Son, so that He would be the firstborn among many brethren; and these whom He predestined, He also called; and these whom He called, He also justified; and these whom He justified, He also glorified."

Notice three things.

First, the audience. Verse 28 names them twice: "those who love God," "those who are called according to His purpose." Paul is not writing about humanity at large. He is writing about Christians — those who already love God, who have been called and have responded. The chain that follows in vv. 29–30 describes the path that Christians have walked, not a sovereign decree concerning all mankind.

Second, the basis of the predestination. Verse 29: "those whom He foreknew, He also predestined." The predestination follows the foreknowledge. God foreknew certain ones, and those He foreknew He predestined to *what*? To "become conformed to the image of His Son." The thing predestined is conformity to Christ. The basis of the predestination is foreknowledge.

Calvinism handles this verse by saying God's "foreknowledge" is just His foreordination by another name — that He foreknew because He had foreordained. But that is not what the word means. *Proginōskō* — to know beforehand — is used elsewhere in the New Testament of human beings knowing things in advance (Acts 26:5; 2 Pet. 3:17). Peter uses the same word of Christ Himself in 1 Peter 1:20: "He was foreknown before the foundation of the world." Christ was not foreordained to exist; He always existed. He was foreknown — known in advance. The same word in Romans 8:29 carries the same meaning. God knew in advance who would respond, who would love Him, who would be in His Son. Those He foreknew, He predestined to become conformed to the image of His Son. The order is foreknowledge first, then predestination — not predestination first, then faith.

Third, the chain. "Those whom He predestined, He also called; and these whom He called, He also justified; and these whom He justified, He also glorified." This is the path of the Christian. Predestined to conformity, called by the gospel, justified by faith, glorified at the end. The text does not say everyone called is justified — many are called and refuse, and the parables are full of them (Matt. 22:14). It says everyone in the chain has been the object of each prior link. Those who are justified were called. Those who were called were predestined to be conformed to Christ. The chain describes those who arrive at glory, not a sealed roster of individuals chosen unconditionally.

## The Impartiality of God

The doctrine of unconditional election cannot be squared with the impartiality of God. Scripture says, repeatedly and directly, that God shows no partiality.

2 Chronicles 19:7 — "with the LORD our God there is no injustice or partiality."

Acts 10:34–35 — "I most certainly understand now that God is not one to show partiality, but in every nation the man who fears Him and does what is right is welcome to Him."

Romans 2:11 — "For there is no partiality with God."

1 Peter 1:17 — "If you address as Father the One who impartially judges according to each one's work."

The conclusion is unavoidable. If God selects some men for salvation and consigns others to damnation without regard to anything in the men themselves — without "any foresight of faith, or good works, or perseverance in either of them," in the Westminster Confession's own words — then His selection is partiality. There is no other word for it. To choose A over B for reasons that have nothing to do with A or B is the very definition of partiality. The Westminster Confession boasts of this — it boasts that the choice rests on God's good pleasure alone, not on anything in the men. Scripture calls that partiality and says God does not do it.

Acts 10:34–35 is especially decisive. Peter is explaining why the gospel has come to Cornelius, a Gentile. He says God shows no partiality, *but* — note the contrast — "in every nation the man who fears Him and does what is right is welcome to Him." The non-partiality of God is grounded in the universal acceptability of those who fear Him and do right, regardless of nation. Two things follow. One: the welcome is universal — every nation. Two: the welcome is conditioned — fear God, do right. Peter does not say God has selected certain Gentiles unconditionally and is now announcing it. He says God welcomes the God-fearing and the upright in every nation, and Cornelius is the proof.

1 Timothy 2:4 says God "desires all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth." 2 Peter 3:9 says "the Lord is… not wishing for any to perish but for all to come to repentance." These are flat statements. God desires all men to be saved. God is not willing that any perish. The Calvinist must read these texts as referring only to "the elect" — only the men God has already chosen — to keep the system intact. But the texts say "all men" and "any." The Calvinist reading turns them into their opposite.

And then there is Matthew 23:37 — Jesus weeping over Jerusalem: "How often I wanted to gather your children together, the way a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, and you were unwilling." Jesus *wanted* to gather them. *They* were unwilling. The will of Christ was for their gathering. Their will was for their refusal. Calvinism cannot accept this verse as it stands. If Christ wanted to gather them, and if Christ's will is sovereign and irresistible, then they would have been gathered. The text says they were unwilling, and that their unwillingness, not Christ's unwillingness, was the obstacle. The will of God for their salvation was real. Their refusal was real. The text places the obstruction on the human side of the relation — exactly where unconditional election cannot place it.

## The Objection from Total Depravity

The Calvinist response to the impartiality argument is usually some version of total depravity: that all men are dead in sin (Eph. 2:1), that no one seeks for God (Rom. 3:11), that the natural man cannot understand the things of the Spirit (1 Cor. 2:14), and therefore that no one can respond to the gospel unless God first regenerates him irresistibly. On this view, election is "unconditional" in the sense that God chooses without regard to anything man does — because man is incapable of doing anything until God first acts on him irresistibly.

The texts cited do say what they say. Men are dead in trespasses and sins until they are made alive in Christ (Eph. 2:1–5). The natural man, apart from divine revelation, does not seek God on his own steam. But these texts do not require irresistible regeneration before any human response. They require that the gospel be preached, that the Spirit work through that gospel, and that man respond.

Romans 10:13–17 settles the order. "Whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved. How then will they call on Him in whom they have not believed? How will they believe in Him whom they have not heard? How will they hear without a preacher?… So faith comes from hearing, and hearing by the word of Christ."

The chain is preacher, then hearing, then faith, then calling, then salvation. Faith comes by hearing the word of Christ. The word, preached, produces faith. There is no irresistible regeneration that precedes hearing in this chain. The Spirit works through the word — not in independent contradiction to the word, not above the word, not beneath it.

This is what Jesus said in John 6:45 — "It is written in the prophets, 'And they shall all be taught of God.' Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father, comes to Me." The drawing of the Father (v. 44) is not by mystical override. It is by teaching. The taught come. The not-taught do not. And Romans 10 specifies the means by which the teaching comes: preachers preaching the word.

Total depravity, as Calvinism uses it, makes man so dead that he cannot respond to a word at all. But Scripture's actual picture of the lost man is different. He is dead in sin — yes, his life is in the wrong place — but he is not dead like a stone. He hears, he understands, he resists, he responds. The Pharisees of John 5 heard Jesus and refused; Jesus told them they were *unwilling*: "you are unwilling to come to Me so that you may have life" (John 5:40). The Athenians of Acts 17 heard Paul; some sneered, some delayed, some believed (Acts 17:32–34). The Bereans heard him and "examined the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so" (Acts 17:11). At every point the response is described as human response — willing or unwilling, examining or refusing, believing or rejecting. The text does not insert a hidden decree behind the response. It treats the response as the response.

## Election in Scripture Is Conditional

If election is not unconditional, what is it? Scripture's answer is that election is corporate, in Christ, conditioned on response.

*Corporate.* God's elect are His people. Israel was elect as a nation (Deut. 7:6–7). The Servant of Isaiah is the Chosen One (Isa. 42:1). Christ, the true Servant, is the Elect of God (Luke 9:35; 1 Pet. 2:4). The church, those who are in Christ, are the elect because they share the chosen status of the Chosen One. 1 Peter 2:9 — "you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God's own possession." Israel's election did not mean every Israelite was saved — Paul makes this explicit in Romans 9:6: "they are not all Israel who are descended from Israel." The election was the nation's; the inclusion of the individual depended on his response.

*In Christ.* The elect of the New Covenant are those in Christ. There is no "elect" status apart from being in Christ. Every blessing in Ephesians 1 is "in Him" or "in Christ" or "in the Beloved" — Paul uses these phrases ten times in twelve verses. The elect are those who are in Christ. The way one becomes "in Christ" is by being placed into Him in baptism (Rom. 6:3; Gal. 3:27 — "all of you who were baptized into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ"), preceded by hearing, believing, repenting, and confessing.

*Conditioned on response.* Every text that names election or chosenness in the New Testament also names conditions. 1 Peter 1:1–2 names the elect: "chosen according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, by the sanctifying work of the Spirit, *to obey Jesus Christ* and be sprinkled with His blood." The elect are chosen *to obey* Christ. Their election is for obedience, not in lieu of it. 2 Peter 1:10 — "Therefore, brethren, be all the more diligent to make certain about His calling and choosing you." Make certain — by your diligence. Calvinism cannot accommodate that imperative; Peter writes it without explanation. 2 Thessalonians 2:13–14 — "God has chosen you from the beginning for salvation through sanctification by the Spirit and faith in the truth. It was for this He called you through our gospel, that you may gain the glory of our Lord Jesus Christ." Chosen for salvation *through* sanctification and faith — *called* through the gospel. The elect are chosen by means of the conditions God has stated.

Election is therefore not the absence of conditions. It is God's eternal plan that those who meet the conditions of being in Christ — hearing, believing, repenting, confessing, being baptized into His death, abiding in Him — would be His chosen ones, holy and blameless before Him, adopted as His sons, sealed with His Spirit, and brought at last to glory.

## Why This Matters

The doctrine of unconditional election does not stay in the seminary. It comes down to the pew, and from the pew it comes down to the conscience.

A man taught unconditional election concludes one of two things. Either he is one of the elect, in which case nothing can be lost; or he is not, in which case nothing can be gained. If he is elect, his obedience is the inevitable fruit of his election, not a condition of his salvation; he need not sweat over it because God will produce it in him. If he is not elect, no obedience can save him; the offer of the gospel was never genuinely for him in the first place. Either way the urgency of conditional response collapses. The man either rests on a status he cannot lose or grieves over a status he cannot gain.

This is precisely what the New Testament does not let men do. "Examine yourselves to see if you are in the faith" (2 Cor. 13:5). "Be all the more diligent to make certain about His calling and choosing you" (2 Pet. 1:10). "Work out your salvation with fear and trembling" (Phil. 2:12). "Take care, brethren, that there not be in any one of you an evil, unbelieving heart that falls away from the living God" (Heb. 3:12). These imperatives presume a man whose response matters, whose continuance matters, whose drift can be fatal and whose endurance can be saving. They presume the conditional structure of the gospel. They are unintelligible on Calvinist premises.

The God of Scripture is not the God of the system. The God of Scripture made man in His image with the capacity to obey or refuse. He has stated His terms — clearly, repeatedly, in both Testaments — and He calls men to meet them. He weeps over those who will not. He rejoices over those who will. He shows no partiality, opens His arms to all who fear Him, and is not willing that any perish. The system describes a different God.

The rest of this book is about the terms God has stated. Lesson by lesson, Charles Willis's workbook gathers the texts that name those terms — the conditions for entering Christ, for remaining in Him, for prayer, for warning, for life. This chapter has cleared the ground. Now we begin to build.
