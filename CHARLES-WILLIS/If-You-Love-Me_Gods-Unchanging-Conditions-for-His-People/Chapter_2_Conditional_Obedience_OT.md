*Chapter 2*

# Conditional Statements About Obedience (Old Testament)

The God whose name has just been cleared of unconditional election is the same God who, from His first words to His first man, has spoken in conditions. Chapter 1 was about clearing the ground — about taking the system that has crowded the conditional language of Scripture out of the modern pulpit and naming it for what it is. The God who came out from under that system was the impartial God, the God who calls all men, the God whose Son weeps over the city that will not come to Him. This chapter takes up what that God said next.

What He said, from Eden onward, was *if*. He said it to Adam in the garden. He said it to Noah after the flood. He said it to Abraham at the cutting of the covenant. He said it to Israel at Sinai. He said it to David about his throne, to Solomon about the temple, to Israel about the land, to the priesthood about the altar, and to the prophets about the people. He said it through Moses, through Joshua, through Samuel, through Isaiah, through Jeremiah, through Ezekiel, through Malachi. He said it in promises and He said it in warnings. He said it where the word *then* appeared and He said it where the word *then* was understood. He said it so often, and in so many places, and in so many forms, that to read the Old Testament without seeing it is to have read past the texture of the book.

The texture of the Old Testament is conditional. That is what this chapter exists to show.

## The First Conditional

Before there was a Law, before there was a covenant called by that name, before there was a chosen nation or a Promised Land or a tabernacle or a priesthood, there was a garden, and a man, and a single sentence from God to that man. That sentence was a conditional. It is the first thing God is recorded as saying to a moral creature.

> Then the LORD God took the man and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate it and keep it. The LORD God commanded the man, saying, "From any tree of the garden you may eat freely; but from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat from it you will surely die." (Gen. 2:15–17)

The structure is unmistakable. Here is the gift: every tree of the garden, freely. Here is the condition: not from this one. Here is the consequence: in the day you eat, you will surely die.

Notice what this verse does not contain. It does not contain a sovereign decree concerning Adam's eternal destiny apart from Adam's response. It does not contain an irresistible enabling of Adam's will. It does not contain a hidden plan that would secure obedience without engaging the man's choice. It contains a command and a warning, addressed to a moral creature capable of obeying or refusing, with the consequence of disobedience stated plainly so the man would know what was at stake.

The reader who comes to Genesis 2 from the system of Chapter 1 has to do considerable work to make this verse fit. If God had decreed Adam's eating from before the foundation of the world, the warning was a ceremony with no substance. If God had decreed Adam's not eating, the warning was unnecessary. The text shows a different picture. God set a real condition before a real man whose response was real, and what followed in Genesis 3 was Adam's response, not the unfolding of a decree.

This is where the Old Testament begins. Not with God's unilateral arrangement of human destinies, but with God's conditional address to a man capable of answer. Every page that follows builds on this beginning.

## The Shape of *If…Then*

Because conditions are the texture of the Old Testament, it is worth pausing to see how the conditional structure works. Charles's workbook opens Lesson 2 by walking the student through ordinary if-then logic — the kind of statement we use every day without reflection — and then showing that the same construction governs God's covenant speech. The point is not that God invented the form. He used it because human beings already use it, because it is how moral commitments and consequences are communicated.

In its simplest form: *If* condition, *then* response. In Genesis 18:26 the LORD says of Sodom, "If I find in Sodom fifty righteous within the city, then I will spare the whole place on their account." The condition is named (fifty righteous). The response is named (sparing the city). If the condition is met, the response follows. If the condition is not met, the response does not follow. There is no third path. The only way Sodom could be spared *on the basis God named* was for the condition God named to be met. It was not met. Sodom was not spared.

Sometimes the word *then* is omitted and the conditional force is carried by the structure alone. "If you will give earnest heed to the voice of the LORD your God, and do what is right in His sight, and give ear to His commandments, and keep all His statutes, *I will put none of the diseases on you which I have put on the Egyptians*" (Ex. 15:26). No *then*. The promise is no less conditioned for the missing word. Sometimes the conditional force is carried by *when* rather than *if*: "You will seek Me and find Me when you search for Me with all your heart" (Jer. 29:13). The *when* of seeking carries the same load as the *if* of obedience. God's being found is conditioned on the wholeheartedness of the search.

Sometimes a single response is conditioned on multiple if-clauses joined together. Jeremiah 7:5–7 names five conditions that together determine one response. "If you truly amend your ways and your deeds, *if* you truly practice justice between a man and his neighbor, *if* you do not oppress the alien, the orphan, or the widow, *and do not* shed innocent blood in this place, *nor walk* after other gods to your own ruin, then I will let you dwell in this place, in the land that I gave to your fathers forever and ever." Five conditions, all required, joined to one promise. Israel did not hear "amend your ways *or* practice justice *or* protect the alien." Israel heard *all* of them, joined.

Sometimes the conditional has both halves stated, blessing for obedience and curse for disobedience, side by side. Isaiah 1:18–20 — "If you consent and obey, you will eat the best of the land; but if you refuse and rebel, you will be devoured by the sword. Truly, the mouth of the LORD has spoken." Both halves are God's word. The blessing is real on its condition. The curse is real on its condition. The choice between them belongs to the hearer.

These are not stylistic curiosities of Hebrew literature. They are the structure of how God communicated His will to His people. The Hebrew conditional particle אִם (*'im*, "if") and its companions אִם־לֹא ("if not"), כִּי ("when"), and the construct waw-perfect carrying conditional force are everywhere in the Pentateuch and the Prophets. To translate the Old Testament without translating the conditional structure would be to flatten the book into something Scripture is not. The God of the Old Testament does not deal with His people unconditionally. He names His terms.

## Sinai: The Covenant That Begins With *If*

The first words God spoke to Israel as a people, when He met them at the foot of Mount Sinai, were a conditional. They were not "you are mine forever and nothing can change that." They were not "I have chosen you irresistibly without regard for anything in you." They were these:

> Now then, if you will indeed obey My voice and keep My covenant, then you shall be My own possession among all the peoples, for all the earth is Mine; and you shall be to Me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation. (Ex. 19:5–6)

Read those two verses with care, because the whole shape of God's relation to Israel is in them. The privilege offered is enormous: God's own possession, set apart from all the peoples of the earth; a kingdom of priests, a holy nation. The privilege is offered — not assumed. The privilege is conditioned — *if you will indeed obey My voice and keep My covenant*. The condition stands before the promise. The promise is real. The condition is real. Israel's status as God's possession was contingent, from the very first words, on their obedience.

This matters because the modern reader has been taught to think of Israel's chosen status as a kind of unconditional ethnic identity, a permanent designation God conferred on Abraham's seed regardless of response. Exodus 19:5–6 says otherwise. Israel was offered the status conditional on obedience. The same verses appear, in transformed form, in 1 Peter 2:9 — "you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God's own possession" — addressed to Christians. The conditional pattern of Sinai becomes the conditional pattern of the church. The privilege is enormous. The privilege is offered. The privilege is conditioned on response.

Sinai's conditionality is not an isolated moment. It runs through the entire Mosaic legislation. In Exodus 23:20–22, just a few chapters later, God promises that the angel He sends will guard Israel and bring them safely to the place He has prepared, *if* they truly obey his voice and do all that He says. "But if you truly obey his voice and do all that I say, then I will be an enemy to your enemies and an adversary to your adversaries." The protection is real. The condition is real. Earlier still, at Marah, the same pattern: "If you will give earnest heed to the voice of the LORD your God, and do what is right in His sight, and give ear to His commandments, and keep all His statutes, I will put none of the diseases on you which I have put on the Egyptians; for I, the LORD, am your healer" (Ex. 15:26). The healing is conditioned on the listening.

There is no point in the Sinai narrative where God says to Israel, "Obey or disobey, it makes no difference. You are mine either way." There is no such verse. The verse that exists, the verse spoken before any other commandment was given, is *if you will indeed obey My voice…then.*

## Deuteronomy: The Covenant Renewed in the Plains of Moab

Forty years later, the generation that came out of Egypt was dead in the wilderness. Moses gathered the next generation on the plains of Moab, just east of the Jordan, to renew the covenant before they crossed into the land. The book of Deuteronomy is essentially that renewal speech — Moses's last words to Israel before he climbed Mount Nebo to die. If any place in the Old Testament concentrates the conditional structure, it is here.

Deuteronomy 6 opens with a command that has come to be known by its first Hebrew word, *Shema* — "Hear":

> Hear, O Israel! The LORD is our God, the LORD is one! You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might. (Deut. 6:4–5)

Notice that the great central confession of Israel's faith is not a description but a command. *Hear.* *You shall love.* The Shema does not tell Israel that they happen to love God; it commands them to. The whole heart, the whole soul, the whole might — total, active, willing devotion. And the chapters that follow this command spell out the conditional structure that depends on whether the command is kept.

A few verses later in Deuteronomy 7, Moses states the covenant in language that resists every doctrine of unconditional election:

> Know therefore that the LORD your God, He is God, the faithful God, who keeps His covenant and His lovingkindness to a thousandth generation with those who love Him and keep His commandments; but repays those who hate Him to their faces, to destroy them; He will not delay with him who hates Him, He will repay him to his face. Therefore, you shall keep the commandment and the statutes and the judgments which I am commanding you today, to do them. (Deut. 7:9–11)

God's covenant lovingkindness extends to a thousand generations, but to a clearly identified group: *those who love Him and keep His commandments*. The faithfulness of God is unfailing toward the obedient. It does not extend the same way toward the disobedient; for them, verse 10, the LORD repays. The "therefore" in verse 11 is not a decorative connector. It is the logical conclusion: because God deals this way with the obedient and that way with the disobedient, *you shall keep the commandment*. The covenant is structured around the response.

A chapter later, in Deuteronomy 8, the same warning takes the form of a stark conditional with the consequence stated plainly:

> It shall come about if you ever forget the LORD your God and go after other gods and serve them and worship them, I testify against you today that you will surely perish. Like the nations that the LORD makes to perish before you, so you shall perish; because you would not listen to the voice of the LORD your God. (Deut. 8:19–20)

Forget the LORD — go after other gods — serve them — worship them: those are the conditions. The response: *you will surely perish.* This is not metaphor. This is the same phrase God used in Eden — "you will surely die" — applied now to a nation rather than a man, but with the same conditional force. The God who made the man and warned him is the God who made the nation and warns it.

In Deuteronomy 11:26–28, Moses puts the choice before Israel as plainly as a choice can be put: "See, I am setting before you today a blessing and a curse: the blessing, if you listen to the commandments of the LORD your God…and the curse, if you do not listen to the commandments of the LORD your God." Two conditions. Two responses. One choice.

By the time Moses reaches the end of his speech, he has compressed the whole covenant into a final summons:

> I call heaven and earth to witness against you today, that I have set before you life and death, the blessing and the curse. So choose life in order that you may live, you and your descendants, by loving the LORD your God, by obeying His voice, and by holding fast to Him; for this is your life and the length of your days. (Deut. 30:19–20)

The witnesses are heaven and earth — Moses is putting the words of God on the cosmic record. The choice is named: life and death, blessing and curse. The means of choosing life is named: loving the LORD, obeying His voice, holding fast to Him. The reason is named: He is your life. There is no second path. There is no fine print that exempts certain Israelites from the choice on grounds of unconditional election. There is the choice, and the witnesses, and the consequence, and the call to choose.

Deuteronomy 4:29–31 adds the assurance that the choice is real for the seeker. "But from there you will seek the LORD your God, and you will find Him if you search for him with all your heart and all your soul." Even the Israelite scattered into exile — even the man under the curse of the covenant for his disobedience — even he, if he searches for God with all his heart, will find God. The condition is the searching with the whole heart. The promise is finding God. God's mercy is not in question. God's mercy is the very framework within which the condition is set.

## The Covenant as Blessing-and-Curse: Leviticus 26

If Deuteronomy concentrates the conditional summons, Leviticus 26 is the structural diagram. The chapter is built as a single, sustained if-then-else covering the entire range of Israel's relationship to God. It begins with the positive condition:

> If you walk in My statutes and keep My commandments so as to carry them out, then I shall give you rains in their season, so that the land will yield its produce and the trees of the field will bear their fruit. (Lev. 26:3–4)

Verses 4–13 unfold the blessing: rain, harvest, peace, the absence of beasts, military safety, fruitfulness, the very presence of God walking among them. *I will be your God, and you shall be My people.* Each blessing is part of the *then* clause. Each blessing depends on the *if*.

Then verse 14 turns the structure over:

> But if you do not obey Me and do not carry out all these commandments, if, instead, you reject My statutes, and if your soul abhors My ordinances so as not to carry out all My commandments, and so break My covenant, I, in turn, will do this to you: I will appoint over you a sudden terror, consumption and fever that will waste away the eyes and cause the soul to pine away; also, you will sow your seed uselessly, for your enemies will eat it up. (Lev. 26:14–16)

Verses 14–39 then unfold the curse — disease, famine, defeat, exile, the land left desolate. The curse escalates in stages, with God repeating "if then you act with hostility against Me and are unwilling to obey Me, I will continue striking you sevenfold for your sins" (v. 21). Each stage is conditional on continued disobedience. Each stage corresponds to the obstinacy of the response.

Two things in this passage repay close attention.

First, the conditions are joined. Verse 14 says, "if you do not obey Me and do not carry out all these commandments." Not most. Not the ones you find palatable. *All.* Verse 15 names a fourfold rejection: rejecting the statutes, the soul abhorring the ordinances, refusing to carry out all the commandments, and breaking the covenant. These are not separable. To reject one part of God's revealed will, with the soul, is to break the covenant. James says the same thing under the New Covenant: "whoever keeps the whole law and yet stumbles in one point, he has become guilty of all" (Jas. 2:10). The law is one fabric. To tear it at one place is to tear it.

Second, the heart is part of the obedience. "If your soul abhors My ordinances" — the soul, the inner person — "so as not to carry out all My commandments." Mechanical compliance with an inwardly resentful spirit is not the obedience God required. Even under the Old Covenant, which is so often caricatured as a covenant of pure external observance, God demanded the willing heart. Leviticus 26:15 condemns the soul that *abhors* God's ordinances — and a soul can abhor what the body still performs. The obedience God required was always whole-person obedience.

The chapter does not end with the curse. Verses 40–45 describe what happens if Israel, under the curse and in exile, confesses iniquity and humbles the uncircumcised heart: "then I will remember My covenant with Jacob, and I will remember also My covenant with Isaac, and My covenant with Abraham as well." Even the worst of the curse — exile — has a way back, and the way back is conditional too. *If* they humble themselves, *then* God remembers. The whole chapter, end to end, is conditional structure. There is no neutral middle.

## The Prophets: Same Conditions, Hundreds of Years Later

Israel's history is a long failure to meet the conditions God stated. The book of Judges traces it cycle by cycle: rebellion, oppression, repentance, deliverance, rest, rebellion again. The kingdom under Saul, David, Solomon, and the divided monarchy under their successors traces it more grandly: the same cycle on a national scale, until at last the curses of Leviticus 26 fall in their fullness. The northern kingdom went into Assyrian exile in 722 B.C. The southern kingdom went into Babylonian exile in 586 B.C. The land lay desolate, exactly as Leviticus 26 had said it would.

Through all of this, the prophets carried the conditional word. They did not soften it. They did not announce that the conditional structure had been replaced with an unconditional grace that overlooked rebellion. They preached the same conditions, and they preached them with the curses on the way.

Jeremiah, prophesying just before and during Jerusalem's fall, spoke at the gate of the temple to a people who had convinced themselves that the temple's presence guaranteed God's protection regardless of their conduct. His message:

> If you truly amend your ways and your deeds, if you truly practice justice between a man and his neighbor, if you do not oppress the alien, the orphan, or the widow, and do not shed innocent blood in this place, nor walk after other gods to your own ruin, then I will let you dwell in this place, in the land that I gave to your fathers forever and ever. (Jer. 7:5–7)

Five conditions, all required, joined to a single promise. The temple did not exempt Jerusalem. The Davidic line did not exempt Jerusalem. The chosen-people status did not exempt Jerusalem. The conditions had to be met. They were not. Jerusalem fell.

Isaiah, more than a century earlier, had pressed the same conditional structure on a Judah still flirting with the same idolatries. "If you consent and obey, you will eat the best of the land; but if you refuse and rebel, you will be devoured by the sword. Truly, the mouth of the LORD has spoken" (Isa. 1:19–20). The closing line — *truly, the mouth of the LORD has spoken* — is God's own seal on both halves of the conditional. The blessing on its condition. The curse on its condition. Both are God's word. To accept one half and refuse the other is to refuse God Himself.

Ezekiel, prophesying to the exiles in Babylon, would put the conditional on the individual scale: "the soul who sins will die" (Ezek. 18:4); but also, "if the wicked man turns from all his sins which he has committed and observes all My statutes and practices justice and righteousness, he shall surely live; he shall not die" (Ezek. 18:21). The conditional is applied man by man, with the reality of individual response held out clearly. Even at the end of the covenant — even with the temple gone and the people scattered — the conditional structure stood.

Jeremiah's word to the exiles in chapter 29 — written to those who had already received the curse, who were living it as he wrote — held out the same conditional promise to the seeker:

> You will seek Me and find Me when you search for Me with all your heart. (Jer. 29:13)

The *when* carries the conditional load. God's being found is conditioned on the wholeheartedness of the search. The seeker who half-searches does not find. The seeker whose heart is divided between God and other gods — the very condition Israel had fallen into for centuries — does not find. The whole-hearted searcher finds. This is not a generic spiritual maxim; it is a covenantal statement, with all the weight of the covenant behind it.

## The Heart Was Always the Issue

It is sometimes argued — by those who want to maintain a sharp split between an Old Testament of works and a New Testament of grace — that the Old Covenant required only external compliance, while the New Covenant first reaches the heart. This is not what the Old Testament says about itself.

From the beginning, the conditions God set were heart conditions. The Shema — Deuteronomy 6:5 — commanded love of God with the whole heart, soul, and might. Deuteronomy 4:29 promised that God would be found by those who searched with all their heart and all their soul. Leviticus 26:15 condemned the soul that abhorred God's ordinances. Joshua at the end of his life called Israel to serve the LORD "in sincerity and truth" (Josh. 24:14). David, instructing Solomon, named the inward part of the condition explicitly: "And as for you, my son Solomon, know the God of your father, and serve Him with a whole heart and a willing mind; for the LORD searches all hearts, and understands every intent of the thoughts. If you seek Him, He will let you find Him; but if you forsake Him, He will reject you forever" (1 Chron. 28:9). *Whole heart. Willing mind. The LORD searches all hearts.* Solomon was not being told to perform the rituals of the temple. He was being told to seek God with the inner person — and the conditional was attached. Seek with the whole heart, and God lets you find Him. Forsake, and God rejects.

Isaiah 1:19, in the conditional already cited, makes the inward disposition part of the condition: "If you consent and obey." *Consent* — the willing inward agreement — comes before *obey*, the outward action. The mechanical performance of commandments without consent of the heart is what Isaiah 1 spends its opening verses denouncing: God is sick of sacrifices offered by hands full of bloodshed (vv. 11–17). The reform demanded is inward and outward together.

This means the New Testament's emphasis on the heart is not a New Testament invention. It is a continuation. When Jesus says, "out of the heart come evil thoughts" (Matt. 15:19), He is sounding a note Moses sounded in Deuteronomy. When Paul says God is not impressed by the outward man but by the heart (Rom. 2:28–29), he is restating what David said to Solomon and what Samuel said to Saul (1 Sam. 16:7). The God who searches the heart in the New Covenant is the God who searched the heart in the Old. The conditions are heart conditions in both Testaments because God is the same God.

## Why This Matters: God Has Not Changed

The point of all this is not historical interest. The point is the through-line. The God who related to His people through stated conditions, through the entire Old Testament, is the same God who relates to His people now. He has not changed. Three witnesses make the point in three different ways.

> For I, the LORD, do not change. (Mal. 3:6)

> Of old You founded the earth, and the heavens are the work of Your hands. Even they will perish, but You endure; and all of them will wear out like a garment; like clothing You will change them and they will be changed. But You are the same, and Your years will not come to an end. (Ps. 102:25–27)

> Every good thing given and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shifting shadow. (Jas. 1:17)

Three texts, three writers, three eras of revelation, one statement: God does not change. The heavens themselves will be folded up like a worn garment. God will not. He is not stricter on Tuesday and gentler on Wednesday. He is not exacting under Moses and easygoing under Christ. He is not fiercer in the Old Testament and softer in the New. The mercies and the requirements alike come from a Father whose character does not move.

It is true that the *covenant* has changed. The writer of Hebrews makes this explicit: the first covenant has become obsolete and is "ready to disappear" (Heb. 8:13); the priesthood has been altered (Heb. 7:12); the sacrifice is no longer the blood of bulls and goats but the blood of Christ (Heb. 10:1–10). The administration is new. The mediator is new. The promises are better. But the God behind the covenant is unchanged, and the God behind the covenant is the God who has always related to His people through stated conditions.

This is the bridge from this chapter to the next. If the texture of God's relation to His people under the Old Covenant was conditional — and we have just walked through fifteen passages that show it was — then we should expect, on the simplest possible inference from God's unchanging character, that the texture of His relation under the New Covenant is conditional also. The next chapter shows that this is exactly what we find. The same God, in the New Testament, speaks in *if* and *when* and *unless* and *whoever* on every page.

## A Note on the Two-Covenant Move

A reader trained in Reformed theology will recognize, at this point, an argument designed to insulate the New Testament from what we have just read. The argument runs like this: *The Old Covenant was a covenant of works for Israel as a national entity, in which conditional blessings and curses governed temporal outcomes in the land. The New Covenant is a covenant of grace, in which salvation is by faith alone, apart from any conditional response. The conditionality of the Old Covenant therefore tells us nothing about the structure of the New.*

This argument is older than it looks. The systematic distinction between a "covenant of works" and a "covenant of grace" was developed in seventeenth-century Reformed federal theology, particularly in Johannes Cocceius's *Summa doctrinae de foedere et testamento Dei* (1648).[^1] Westminster's chapter VII codified it for the English-speaking Reformed tradition. The vocabulary is not Scripture's. *Covenant of works* and *covenant of grace* do not appear in any biblical text. They are theological constructions imposed on the text in order to organize it within a particular system.

That, by itself, is not an argument against them. Theological constructions are sometimes useful. The argument against this particular construction is that it does not match what Scripture itself says about the relationship between the covenants and the people who lived under them.

First: the writer of Hebrews places Old Testament saints in a single line of faith with New Testament Christians. Hebrews 11 lists Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses, Rahab, Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel, and the prophets — all "men of old [who] gained approval" by faith (Heb. 11:2), all "having gained approval through their faith" (v. 39). Their faith was not a different commodity from the faith required of Christians. They are not described as participants in a covenant of works who happened to be saved by some separate arrangement. They are described as believers in the same line of faith that culminates in Christ. Romans 4 makes the same point about Abraham: he was justified by faith, as Christians are.

Second: the faith that justified Abraham was an obedient faith. Genesis 22 records God's call to Abraham to offer Isaac, and Abraham's obedient response. James 2:21 says, "Was not Abraham our father justified by works when he offered up Isaac his son on the altar?" — and verse 23 quotes Genesis 15:6 as fulfilled in this obedient act: "Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness." James does not separate Abraham's faith from his obedience. They are one thing. The "faith of Abraham" that Paul commends in Romans 4 is the same faith James describes in James 2 — a faith that listened to God and obeyed.

Third: the New Covenant is not less demanding than the Old. The Sermon on the Mount, the constitutional document of the kingdom, intensifies the Law's demands rather than relaxing them. *You have heard…but I say to you* — and what follows is not a softer ethic but a deeper one, reaching into anger, lust, oath-taking, the love of enemies. The New Covenant requires a righteousness that "surpasses that of the scribes and Pharisees" (Matt. 5:20). The conditions have not been removed; they have been clarified and extended into the heart, exactly where the Old Testament always located them.

Fourth: Galatians 3:24–25 calls the Law "our tutor to lead us to Christ, so that we may be justified by faith. But now that faith has come, we are no longer under a tutor." Paul does not say the Law was an alien arrangement to be discarded. He says it was a tutor — a *paidagōgos*, the household servant who walked the child to school — whose function was to bring the people to Christ. The conditional structure of the Law was never separable from the character of God. It was God's instruction to a people not yet ready for the Mediator who would write the law on their hearts. When the Mediator came, the conditional structure was not abolished; it was transposed into the law of Christ (Gal. 6:2; 1 Cor. 9:21).

The two-covenant move, then, cannot do the work it is asked to do. The God of both covenants is the same God. The faith of both covenants is the same faith. The conditions of both covenants are stated by the same lips. What changed at Calvary was the means of atonement, the priesthood, the administration, the indwelling of the Spirit. What did not change was the God who states His terms and calls His people to meet them.

## Looking Forward

This chapter has walked through fifteen Old Testament passages that name conditions, with their responses, with their warnings, in their full force. The pattern is uniform. From Eden to the end of the Hebrew canon, God speaks to His people in conditions. He sets blessings on the obedient and curses on the disobedient. He requires the heart, not just the hand. He warns. He waits. He sometimes takes hundreds of years before the curse falls, but the curse falls. He never tells His people that the conditions do not really apply, that some have been chosen unconditionally and others have been rejected unconditionally and that response is therefore irrelevant. He says: *if* you obey, *then*. *If* you forsake, *then*. *Choose life.*

The next chapter takes up the same pattern in the New Testament. The reader who has begun to suspect that the conditional structure of the Old is the conditional structure of the gospel will find, in the words of Jesus, in the letters of Paul, in the writings of John, in Hebrews, in James, in Peter, the same God speaking the same way to the same kind of people. The same *if*. The same *whoever*. The same warning. The same heart.

Charles Willis's workbook collects those texts. The next chapter walks through them in prose. Together they make the case that the gospel of Christ is structured exactly the way the covenant of Sinai was structured — not because it is a new law, not because it is a covenant of works, but because the God behind both covenants is the same God, and that God speaks in conditions because that is how He has always related to creatures made in His image with the capacity to obey or refuse.

He has not changed. We should not expect Him to.

[^1]: Johannes Cocceius (1603–1669), *Summa doctrinae de foedere et testamento Dei* ("A Summary of the Doctrine of the Covenant and Testament of God"), first published in Leiden in 1648. Cocceius is generally credited as the principal architect of seventeenth-century Reformed federal (covenant) theology, in which the history of God's dealings with men is organized under two principal covenants: a *foedus operum* (covenant of works) made with Adam, and a *foedus gratiae* (covenant of grace) made in Christ. The *Westminster Confession of Faith* (1646), Chapter VII, gives the structure its English-speaking Reformed form. The construction is post-apostolic and the vocabulary is not Scripture's; it is, however, treated by Reformed dogmatics as the framework within which the conditional structures of the Old Testament are insulated from the structure of New Testament salvation. The argument of this chapter (and of Chapter 3) is that the framework will not bear the weight Reformed theology asks it to bear.

## Notes for review (Paul's eye particularly wanted)

Three places where a second look might be useful:

1. **The two-covenant section near the end.** I added a sustained engagement with the federal-theology distinction between covenant of works and covenant of grace because the chapter's whole argument depends on God's unchanging character carrying the conditions across the testaments — and Reformed theology has a developed answer to that. I want to make sure the engagement is fair to the position even as it pushes back, and that it is not so long that it unbalances the chapter. It currently runs about 600 words; if it should be shorter, it's the natural place to compress.

2. **Genesis 2:15–17 as "the first conditional."** I am treating the prohibition on the tree as the first instance of God's conditional speech to a moral creature. This is exegetically defensible (it is the first divine command recorded after man's creation, and it has the structure of conditional warning), but a careful reader might point out that Genesis 1:28 ("be fruitful and multiply…") is technically earlier and not in conditional form. I chose to focus on 2:17 because the conditional structure is explicit there, but flagging in case you want me to treat the relationship between 1:28 and 2:17 directly.

3. **The Cocceius footnote.** The history is accurate, but if the chapter ends up running long in editing, this footnote could be trimmed to two sentences without losing the substance. I left it longer because the federal-theology framework is a real opponent of the chapter's argument and naming it precisely seemed worth the room.

Total length is approximately 5,200 words, in the range of Chapter 1.
