# Book Proposal

## Change the Mind, Change the Man
### A Scripture-Based Journey Through Addiction, Recovery, and the Turning of the Mind

**Author:** Paul Hainline
**Word Count:** ~45,000
**Category:** Christian Nonfiction / Addiction & Recovery / Bible Study
**Status:** Complete manuscript available

---

## Overview

*Change the Mind, Change the Man* is a 10-chapter book about addiction, recovery, and the power of Scripture to do what no program, no willpower, and no change of circumstance can do on its own: change the mind.

The book is written by a man who was introduced to drugs at fourteen, arrested for robbery and murder at seventeen, and sentenced to life in prison — who served thirty-three years and found his way through the valley, not around it, by the grace of God and the honest study of His Word.

It is written *for* the addict. It is written *for* the family — the mother who asks "Where did we go wrong?", the father who cannot sleep, the spouse who smiles on Sunday and falls apart on Monday, the child who heard the crying through the wall. It is written for the friend, the preacher, the counselor, the person who picked up this book because someone they love is in trouble and they don't know what to do.

Every chapter speaks to all of them at once. Because addiction is a shared story, and the road through it is walked together.

The book's central thesis — *change the mind, change the man* — comes from the teaching of Freddie Anderson, a preacher who never answered a question without opening the Bible first. The Greek word for repentance, *metanoia*, is itself a compound of *meta* (change) and *nous* (mind). God chose a word about the mind. The apostle Paul built an entire theology around the renewal of the mind (Romans 12:2). And this book applies that theology directly, carefully, and honestly to the reality of addiction — without flinching, without offering cheap comfort, and without pretending the answers are easy.

## Target Audience

**Primary:**
- Families of addicts — parents, spouses, siblings, children — who are living in the crisis right now and searching for answers rooted in Scripture rather than secular psychology or 12-step methodology
- Addicts themselves, especially those who grew up in faith or who are open to a Bible-based perspective
- Church leaders and preachers who counsel families dealing with addiction and need a resource grounded in Scripture

**Secondary:**
- Recovery ministry leaders (church-based recovery programs, prison ministries, rehab chaplains)
- Christian counselors seeking a supplemental resource that treats addiction through a biblical lens
- General Christian readers drawn to honest personal testimony combined with rigorous Bible study

**Market size:** The National Survey on Drug Use and Health reports that 46.3 million Americans aged 12 and older met the criteria for a substance use disorder in 2021. Each of those individuals has a family. The audience for this book is not niche — it is vast, and it is underserved by the current Christian publishing landscape.

## Comparable Titles and Market Position

**1. *Beautiful Boy* by David Sheff** (Mariner Books, 2008) — A father's memoir of his son's methamphetamine addiction. *New York Times* bestseller. Sheff's book resonated because it told the *family's* story, not just the addict's. *Change the Mind, Change the Man* occupies the same emotional territory — speaking directly to the family — but from a completely different foundation: Scripture rather than secular narrative, and from the perspective of the addict himself looking back with fifty years of distance.

**2. *The Prodigal God* by Timothy Keller** (Penguin, 2008) — Keller's examination of the parable of the prodigal son as a lens for understanding grace. *Change the Mind, Change the Man* draws heavily on the prodigal son narrative (Chapters 7-8), but applies it specifically to addiction and recovery rather than as a general theological framework. Readers who responded to Keller's treatment of the parable will find a raw, lived application of it here.

**3. *Addiction and Grace* by Gerald May** (HarperOne, 1988) — A psychiatrist's theological exploration of addiction and spiritual desire. Influential in its time but clinical in tone. *Change the Mind, Change the Man* is not written by a clinician. It is written by a man who lived it — and who grounds every claim in specific Scripture passages with careful attention to the original Greek, not in psychological theory.

**4. *Unashamed* by Lecrae** (B&H Publishing, 2016) — Grammy-winning artist's memoir of overcoming abuse, addiction, and identity crisis through faith. Lecrae's book succeeded because of raw honesty combined with a faith-based framework. *Change the Mind, Change the Man* shares that raw honesty but goes deeper into systematic Bible study — examining the Greek text of James, Romans, Luke, Hebrews, and Ephesians to build a theological framework for understanding how and why the mind changes.

**What makes this book unique:** There is currently no well-known title that combines (a) firsthand addiction and criminal testimony, (b) rigorous Scripture study including Greek word analysis, and (c) direct, simultaneous address to both the addict and the family in every chapter. The existing market divides into secular memoir, clinical/psychological approaches, 12-step Christian recovery, or devotional encouragement. *Change the Mind, Change the Man* occupies a space none of those categories fill.

## Chapter Summaries

### Chapter 1: The Phone Call
*The moment everything splits into before and after.*

Opens with the universal experience of the family's dreaded phone call — the hospital, the police officer, the text that says "call me now." Establishes that the book speaks to both the addict and the family, and introduces the author's own story (drugs at 14, arrested at 17, 33 years served). Grounds the entire book in Psalm 23:4 and the word *through* — the valley is real, the shadow is real, but the valley has an exit, and the Shepherd is already there.

### Chapter 2: The Progression
*How the mind turns — one step at a time.*

Traces the anatomy of temptation through James 1:14-15 with detailed Greek word study: *exelkomenos* (drawn out — a hunting term), *deleazomenos* (enticed — a fishing term), *syllabousa* (conceived — a biological term). Maps this progression onto the reality of addiction — the first choice, the craving disguised as a choice, the gestation period, the full maturity of sin, and the death it produces. Paralleled with the author's own progression from age 14 to 17. Introduces Romans 1:18-32 as the theological engine of addiction: the mind that turns from God creates a vacuum that must be filled.

### Chapter 3: Where Did We Go Wrong?
*The question that keeps the family awake — and the answer no one wants to hear.*

Addresses the family's guilt directly. Opens with a mother's viral social media post written while driving her son to rehab. Examines the limits of parental responsibility through Ezekiel 18:20 ("The soul who sins will die. The son will not bear the punishment for the father's iniquity") and Deuteronomy 6:6-7. Confronts the hard truth: good parents can do everything right and still watch a child choose destruction — because every human being has free will, and free will is the most dangerous and most sacred thing God ever gave us.

### Chapter 4: All of the Imprisoned Are Not in Prison
*Not every prison has walls you can see.*

Examines the prisons shame builds around the family — the silence, the isolation, the fear of judgment from the church and the community. Draws on the author's experience of literal imprisonment to illuminate the metaphorical imprisonment of the family. Grounds the discussion in Psalm 142 (David's cry from the cave), 2 Corinthians 1:3-4 (the God of all comfort), and the reality that shame thrives in silence and dies in the light.

### Chapter 5: Love That Says No
*The hardest thing a family ever does.*

Confronts enabling — the well-meaning help that keeps the addict comfortable in their chains. Examines the tension between the instinct to rescue and the recognition that rescuing may be the very thing preventing change. Grounds the discussion in Hebrews 12:5-11 (God's discipline as evidence of love, not absence of it), the distinction between discipline and punishment, and the painful reality that sometimes the most loving thing a family can do is let the floor hit. Draws on the author's observation of men who served their time and walked out unchanged because nothing inside had changed.

### Chapter 6: Think
*The chapter about the mind — the real battlefield.*

The theological center of the book. Introduces Freddie Anderson's core teaching: "If you change a person's mind, you change everything about them." Unpacks Romans 12:2 ("be transformed by the renewing of your mind") with detailed study of *nous* (mind), *metamorphoō* (transformed — the root of metamorphosis), and *anakainōsis* (renewal). Examines the swept house of Matthew 12:43-45 as a warning: an empty mind will be refilled. The mind must be occupied, not merely vacated. The question is not whether you will think — the question is *what* you will think about. Anchored in Philippians 4:8 and 2 Corinthians 10:5.

### Chapter 7: Coming to Himself
*What happens when the mind actually turns.*

The prodigal son in the pig field (Luke 15:17) — *eis heauton de elthōn*, "having come to himself." He did not discover new information; he returned to information he had been running from. Carefully distinguishes *metanoia* (change of mind — true repentance) from *metamelomai* (regret — worldly sorrow) through 2 Corinthians 7:10. Paralleled with the author's own turning — years of bargaining, the attempt to disprove God through a geology class, the quiet voice that would not stop, the denied parole hearings, and the eventual reckoning with James 4:14 ("you are just a vapor").

### Chapter 8: The Father Ran
*What happens when the addict comes home.*

The prodigal's return (Luke 15:20-24) and the father's response — he ran to meet him. Examines forgiveness and its limits: forgiveness and trust are not the same thing, and both are necessary but on different timelines. Trust is rebuilt through sustained evidence, not promised in a single conversation. Addresses the older brother (Luke 15:25-32) — the family member who did everything right and now resents the celebration. The gospel's scandal is that the father's love is not proportional to the son's performance.

### Chapter 9: The Long Road
*The daily, unglamorous work of living differently.*

Recovery is not a moment; it is a road made of mornings. Grounds the discussion in Hebrews 12:1 ("let us run with endurance the race that is set before us"), Philippians 3:13-14 (forgetting what lies behind, reaching forward), and the practical reality that faithfulness is built in the ordinary hours. Addresses relapse honestly — the difference between stumbling and turning back, and the instruction of 1 John 1:9. Discusses the role of the church community (Hebrews 10:24-25) not as a support group but as the body of Christ, designed for exactly this.

### Chapter 10: The God Who Finds You
*The invitation that underlies everything.*

The final chapter steps back from addiction to ask the foundational question: Where do you stand with God? Traces the New Testament pattern — hearing (Romans 10:17), belief (John 8:24), repentance (Acts 2:38), confession (Romans 10:9-10), baptism (Galatians 3:27, Romans 6:3-4) — with careful attention to the Greek preposition *eis* (into, movement from one location to another). Every spiritual blessing is *in Christ* (Ephesians 1:3). Baptism is the door *into* Christ. Addresses the common objection about the thief on the cross through Hebrews 9:16-17 (the will is not in force while the testator lives). Ends where it began — with Psalm 23:4, the valley, and the word *through*.

## About the Author

Paul Hainline was introduced to drugs at age fourteen. By seventeen, the progression of addiction had led to robbery and murder, and he was sentenced to life in prison. He served thirty-three years before parole was granted. During those decades, after trying self-help, secular philosophy, and his own reasoning, he returned to the faith he had been raised in — not through a program or a dramatic conversion experience, but through the honest study of Scripture, guided by the teaching of Freddie Anderson, a preacher whose method was to open the Bible and let it answer.

Paul is the author of eight completed books published through NobleMind Press (noblemind.study), covering subjects from prayer (*A New and Living Way*) to evangelism (*Bridge Moments*) to the character of Christ (*The Character No One Could Invent*) to grief (*Through the Valley*) to guides for young men and women (*Your Name Means Everything: A Good Name* and *Strength and Dignity*, co-authored with his wife, Pam Hainline). He also created the Noble Mind Study Tool, a free, open-source Bible study application used by students of Scripture worldwide.

Paul and Pam live in New Caney, Texas. He is sixty-five years old and an active member of the New Caney Church of Christ.

He does not hold degrees in theology, psychology, or any clinical discipline. He holds thirty-three years of lived experience, a changed mind, and an open Bible.
