## THESIS

Until a person's focus is redirected to God — and they are convinced it should *remain* on God — remission from addiction is not just possible, but probable. The descent into addiction is the story of a mind turning away from God. The restoration is the story of a mind turning back. The substance was never the real problem. The gaze was.

---

## STRUCTURAL FRAMEWORK

### The Two Movements

The book follows two movements that mirror the actual experience of addiction and recovery:

**The Descent (Chapters 1–5):** The mind turns from God. Each chapter traces one stage of the turning away — the crisis, the progression, the guilt, the isolation, and the agonizing decisions families are forced to make. These chapters speak to both the addict and the family simultaneously, because both are living inside the same story.

**The Return (Chapters 6–10):** The mind turns back. The hinge is Chapter 6 — THINK — which establishes the biblical mechanism of change. From there, the book traces repentance, forgiveness, the long daily road of recovery, and finally, the gospel invitation for anyone who reaches the end of the book and realizes they need the foundation it describes.

### Voice

Single voice that speaks to both audiences — the addict and the family — simultaneously throughout. Not written TO one with asides to the other. One voice, one narrative, speaking to the whole household. Addiction is a shared experience, and splitting the audiences concedes that they are in different stories. They are not.

### Tone

Let the Scripture set the tone chapter by chapter. Some texts are warm (the prodigal son). Some are sober (Galatians 6:1). Some are hard-edged before they are tender (1 Corinthians 5). The book follows the emotional register of the passage it is working with, just as the experience of addiction itself moves between tenderness, anger, grief, confrontation, and hope.

### Approach

Same core principles as *One Day Closer to Home*:

- Scripture interprets Scripture
- NASB preferred
- Greek/Hebrew woven naturally when it illuminates meaning
- Honest handling — if a passage is hard, say so
- Denominational neutrality
- Conversational, not academic
- Sensory and physical — put the reader *there*
- Direct address ("you")

### Author's Position

Paul speaks to the addict from lived experience: introduced to drugs at age 13, arrested for robbery and murder at 17, served 33 years in prison, now 65. He has walked the full progression — the first choice, the changed associations, the escalation, the consequences, the false starts at repentance, and the genuine turning that changed everything. He writes not from theory, not from a safe distance, but from the road itself.

## PART ONE — THE DESCENT: THE MIND TURNING AWAY

---

### Chapter 1 — The Phone Call

**Theme:** The moment of crisis — when the family discovers the truth, or when the addict hits the floor.

**Origin:** This chapter is born from the viral post on X (March 13, 2026) by a mother driving her 24-year-old son to rehab. Her words — "Where did we go wrong?" and "We never imagined drugs would touch our lives" — are the words of every family that has lived this moment. The post received over 50,000 views and 1,200 replies, confirming that this is a pain families are carrying in silence.

**For the family:** The world just split into before and after. There is the life you had yesterday, and there is the life you have now, and they are not the same life. The shock, the disbelief, the desperate replaying of every memory looking for the sign you missed.

**For the addict:** You may not remember your rock bottom the way your family does. But they remember. They remember exactly where they were standing, what time of day it was, and what it felt like when the floor dropped out.

**"How could you?"** When the addiction has led to a serious crime — especially a violent one — the family's crisis carries an additional weight. "Where did we go wrong?" is the family looking inward. "How could you?" is the family looking directly at the addict, and there is grief in it, but there is also horror. Disbelief. The realization that the person they raised, the person they loved, is capable of something they never imagined. For some families, "How could you?" *is* the phone call. It is not just discovering the addiction. It is discovering what the addiction led to. This thread will return in Chapters 8 and 9, because this question does not resolve quickly — if it resolves at all.

**Paul's story enters here:** Not the full journey yet — just the establishing detail. The reader learns early that the author is not writing from the outside. "I have walked the same path your son is walking."

**Scripture possibilities:** May open with narrative rather than text, similar to Chapter 1 of *One Day Closer to Home*. Psalm 23:4 as an anchor — "Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death" — with emphasis on the word *through*. The valley has a "through." You are not stuck.

### Chapter 2 — The Progression

**Theme:** How addiction develops — the slow turn of the mind away from God.

This chapter answers the question the addict is afraid to ask and the family can't stop asking: *How did this happen?* Not clinically, but honestly. The answer is a progression, and it follows a pattern that Scripture describes long before modern psychology gave it a name.

**The question underneath:** "Why even consent to try it?" Paul is honest that he doesn't fully know. Perhaps something was missing. Perhaps there were no firm moral boundaries at that age. Perhaps a combination. The honesty of not having a neat answer is more valuable than a manufactured one.

**The progression:**
- The first choice — someone introduces you to it, and you say yes
- You like the effects, so you do it again
- The people you associate with change
- You can see how far you've fallen — the embarrassment of running into old friends is real and humiliating — and yet there is no urge to stop
- The escalation: lying, stealing, and worse, because the substance has become more important than anything else
- "I didn't like who I had become better than the person I once was, or I wouldn't have been embarrassed by my appearance. And I don't just mean my physical appearance, although there was that too."

**The theological key:** When you take your focus off of God, you naturally become more self-centered. This is not just an observation about addiction — this is Romans 1. The mind drifts from God, the self fills the vacuum, and the substance fills the emptiness the self creates. The issue was never fundamentally about the drug. It was about where the gaze was fixed.

**Scripture:**
- Romans 1:21-25 — the progression of exchanging God's truth for a lie
- Proverbs 13:20 — "He who walks with wise men will be wise, but the companion of fools will suffer harm" (the changed associations)
- Romans 6:16 — "Do you not know that when you present yourselves to someone as slaves for obedience, you are slaves of the one whom you obey?"

**For the family:** This chapter helps you understand the progression without excusing it. Understanding *how* is not the same as saying it was inevitable.

**For the addict:** This chapter tells your story honestly. No sugarcoating, no clinical detachment. You know this road. So does the author.

---

### Chapter 3 — Where Did We Go Wrong?

**Theme:** Guilt — the family's guilt ("What did we miss?") and the addict's guilt ("How did I become this?")

**Key theological move:** Ezekiel 18:20 — "The son will not bear the punishment for the father's iniquity, nor will the father bear the punishment for the son's iniquity." Each soul is responsible for its own choices. This text simultaneously relieves the parents' guilt and places responsibility honestly on the addict — without cruelty to either.

**For the family:** You didn't cause this. Good parents, loving homes, faithful upbringings — and the child still chose the path. Because they are a person with agency, not a product you manufactured.

**For the addict:** Nobody made you do this. Not your parents, not your circumstances, not your pain. You chose it. And that is actually good news — because if you can choose it, you can choose to stop.

**Honest handling of Proverbs 22:6:** "Train up a child in the way he should go, even when he is old he will not depart from it." Every parent of an addict has had this verse quoted at them, usually unhelpfully. The honest handling: Proverbs are wisdom literature — general principles, not unconditional guarantees. The same book of Proverbs assumes the possibility of a foolish son (Proverbs 17:25). Paul himself was raised in a faithful home, received a solid Christian upbringing, and still chose the path he chose. The verse is true as a principle. It is not a promise that eliminates the child's free will.

**Also honest:** Some families DID fail in real ways — absent parents, broken homes, abuse. The chapter acknowledges that without letting it become an excuse. Circumstances explain; they do not justify.

**Scripture:**
- Ezekiel 18:20 (primary)
- Proverbs 22:6 (addressed directly)
- Proverbs 17:25 — "A foolish son is a grief to his father and bitterness to her who bore him."
- Deuteronomy 24:16 — "Fathers shall not be put to death for their sons, nor shall sons be put to death for their fathers; everyone shall be put to death for his own sin."

---

### Chapter 4 — All of the Imprisoned Aren't in Prison

**Theme:** The hidden prisons — isolation, shame, and the broadening of the audience beyond what people typically picture when they hear "addiction."

**Freddie Anderson's statement** opens this chapter: "All of the imprisoned aren't in prison." This single line does essential work — it blows the walls off the assumption that addiction is someone else's problem, happening in someone else's world.

**The hidden prisons:**
- The person chained to a bottle in a nice house in the suburbs — imprisoned
- The wife who can't tell anyone at church what's happening at home — imprisoned
- The parents smiling on Sunday morning while their child is destroying himself — imprisoned
- The man who has never touched an illegal substance but is enslaved to pornography, gambling, anger, or any other thing that has mastered him — imprisoned
- The family hiding from the church, from friends, from extended family — imprisoned by shame

**For the addict:** Addiction thrives in secrecy. The hiding IS the progression continuing. Every secret kept is another wall built around your cell.

**For the family:** You hide because you are afraid of judgment. You smile on Sunday and fall apart on Monday. You don't know who to tell or how to say it.

**For the church:** What happens when a family is brave enough to say it out loud? Does the body of Christ become a hospital for the wounded — or a courtroom for the accused? This chapter speaks directly to the church about what should happen when someone asks for help.

**Scripture:**
- John 8:34 — "Everyone who commits sin is the slave of sin."
- 2 Peter 2:19 — "For by what a man is overcome, by this he is enslaved."
- James 5:16 — "Confess your sins to one another, and pray for one another so that you may be healed." The connection between confession, community, and healing.
- Psalm 34:18 — "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves those who are crushed in spirit."
- Galatians 6:2 — "Bear one another's burdens, and thereby fulfill the law of Christ."

**Broadening the audience:** The working title mentions "one of man's greatest enemies." This chapter makes clear that the enemy wears many faces. Drugs, alcohol, pornography, gambling — the substances and behaviors vary, but the pattern is the same. The person who picks up this book might not think of themselves as an addict. But they may be imprisoned. And they know it, even if they've never said it out loud.

### Chapter 5 — Love That Says No

**Theme:** The enabling question — where does love end and enabling begin?

**This is the hardest chapter for families.** The instinct is to protect, to rescue, to cushion every fall. But sometimes love looks like a closed door.

**Scripture:** 1 Corinthians 5:1-5 — the Corinthian church was told to remove the sinning man, "so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus." The purpose was redemptive, not punitive. The removal was an act of love aimed at awakening.

**For the family:** You are not abandoning your child when you stop funding their destruction. You are not failing them when you set a boundary. Sometimes the most loving thing you can do is let them feel the full weight of their choices.

**For the addict:** The people who say no to you are not your enemies. They may be the only ones loving you enough to let you hurt.

**Honest handling:** This is where the book must be careful not to become formulaic. Every situation is different. Some boundaries save lives; some bridges need to stay open. Wisdom is required, not a checklist.

**Also:** Hebrews 12:5-11 — God's discipline as a father disciplines a son. The purpose is "the peaceful fruit of righteousness." If God Himself loves through discipline, then discipline can be an act of love, not abandonment.

## PART TWO — THE RETURN: THE MIND TURNING BACK

---

### Chapter 6 — Think

**Theme:** The mechanism of change — the mind. This is the hinge chapter of the entire book.

**This chapter is built on the teaching of Freddie Anderson**, the preacher who had the most profound impact on Paul's life. Freddie's method was simple and consistent: when someone asked a question, he would say, "That's a good question — let's see what the Bible says." Every time. Never what he thought, never what some scholar said, never a commentary — always, "Let's see what the Bible says." That method didn't just teach Paul content; it taught him how to recognize error when he heard it. It taught him to let Scripture interpret Scripture, to call Bible things by Bible names, and to speak where the Bible speaks and be silent where the Bible is silent.

**Freddie's lesson on THINK** (from Philippians 4:8) provides the biblical framework: Paul the apostle, more than any other New Testament writer, emphasizes the *mind*. The word "mind" — as intent, attitude, will, understanding — runs through every letter he wrote. Freddie's argument: if you change a person's mind, you change everything about them. If you don't change their mind, you don't change anything.

**Key texts traced through Paul's letters:**
- Philippians 4:8 — "Whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is right, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is of good repute, if there is any excellence and if anything worthy of praise, dwell on these things."
- Romans 12:1-2 — "Be transformed by the renewing of your mind."
- Philippians 2:2 — "Being of the same mind, maintaining the same love, united in spirit, intent on one purpose."
- Philippians 2:5-8 — "Have this attitude in yourselves which was also in Christ Jesus..."
- Colossians 3:1-2 — "Keep seeking the things above, where Christ is... Set your mind on the things above, not on the things that are on earth."

**The connection to addiction:** This is where Paul's personal experience and Freddie's teaching converge. Freddie said: you can take a convict out of prison, put him in new clothes, put him in the best situation — and he'll go right back. But when you change his mind, it doesn't matter what his circumstances are. He's not going back. Paul lived this: "Your circumstances have changed, but not your outlook. Not your thinking, and not your heart." Prison didn't change Paul. Rehab alone wouldn't have changed Paul. The *mind* had to change. And the mind is changed by fixing it on God.

**For the addict:** No program, no facility, no change of environment will hold if your mind doesn't change. You aren't going to change until you make up your mind to change. And Paul is honest: many go to rehab for others, or to escape their current situation, but not because they have a sincere desire to put the addiction behind them. And as a result, they return to it — over and over again. Even when it lands them in prison. As soon as they get out, they go right back. Because the mind never changed.

**For the family:** This is perhaps the hardest truth for the family to accept — that you cannot change your loved one's mind for them. You can create conditions. You can remove obstacles. You can pray. But the turning of the mind is a choice only they can make.

**The thesis of the book lives here:** Until a person's focus is redirected to God and they are convinced it should *remain* on God, remission is not just possible, but probable. The gaze must be fixed on Christ and on things above.

### Chapter 7 — Coming to Himself

**Theme:** Repentance — the real thing, not the performance.

**Primary text:** Luke 15:17 — "But when he came to his senses..." The prodigal's turning point.

**Key distinction:** The difference between "I'm sorry I got caught" and "I'm sorry I did this." Between remorse over consequences and repentance over sin. Families have been burned by false repentance. Addicts have performed it. The text helps both sides recognize the real thing.

**Paul's turning:** Paul's own story enters with full weight here. He knew God was real. He acknowledged it. But he bargained: "I'll turn back to Him when I get out of prison." Then, after 20 years, denied parole, then denied again, he realized he might never get out. He might wait too long. The folly of "waiting for the right time" became clear. James 4:14 — "You do not know what your life will be like tomorrow." He took a hard look at his life — the things he had done and where he was — and said *enough*.

**The role of upbringing:** Paul thanks God that as a child he had parents who believed, that his family was active in the church community, and that he received a solid Christian upbringing and education. He believes that mattered — not initially, but eventually. That foundation didn't prevent the fall, but it was still there when he was finally ready to come back to it.

**Honest handling of the detours:** Before Paul returned to Scripture, he tried other paths. Self-help books. College geology that told him the earth was billions of years old, and he rejoiced that the Bible couldn't possibly be right. He wanted it to not be true. But there was a still, small voice in the back of his mind asking: "Really? Are you absolutely sure about that?" And over time, he had to acknowledge that he believed there really was a God, that the God of the Bible was real — and what did that mean for him?

**For the addict:** You can't negotiate your way out of this. You can't manage it. You can't moderate it. You have to turn. All the way around. And the turn isn't a moment — it's a direction. And you may not get the tomorrow you're counting on to make that turn.

**For the family:** You will want to believe every promise. You've been hurt before. This chapter helps discern genuine change from manipulation — with compassion for how desperately the family wants it to be real.

**Scripture:**
- Luke 15:17 (primary)
- James 4:14 — "You do not know what your life will be like tomorrow."
- Psalm 51 — David's prayer of repentance. Raw, honest, no excuses.
- Romans 7:15-20 — "The good that I want, I do not do, but I practice the very evil that I do not want." Every addict knows this war.
- 1 John 1:9 — "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and righteous to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness."

### Chapter 8 — The Father Ran

**Theme:** What happens when the addict comes home — return, forgiveness, restoration.

**Primary text:** Luke 15:20-24 — the father saw him "while he was still a long way off" and ran.

**For the family:** The father didn't wait for the son to clean up first. He didn't demand an explanation before he embraced him. He ran. But he also didn't pretend nothing had happened — the son was dead and is alive; was lost and is found. Forgiveness acknowledges the gravity of what happened.

**For the addict:** You don't have to have the perfect speech. The father wasn't listening to the speech. He was looking at the direction of travel.

**The older brother:** Luke 15:25-32. This is in the text for a reason. Not everyone in the family will celebrate. Some will resent. Some will feel that grace is unfair. That's in the story, and it has to be in the book.

**The victims:** This chapter must also address the reality that addiction creates victims — people who were harmed by the addict's choices. Forgiveness is a biblical command, but it does not erase consequences, and it does not mean that every relationship can be restored to what it was before. Some damage is permanent in this life. The book must be honest about that.

**"How could you?" returns here.** The family asked this question in the crisis of Chapter 1. It has not gone away. Forgiveness may have been given, but "How could you?" doesn't always disappear just because forgiveness has arrived. For the addict, there is no answer that satisfies — not for the family, and not for the addict himself. By the time you are capable of understanding *why* you did it, you are a different person than the one who did it, and that earlier version of yourself is almost incomprehensible to you now. And yet the question remains. The David connection is essential here: after his sin with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah, David was forgiven — Nathan told him directly in 2 Samuel 12:13, "The LORD also has taken away your sin; you shall not die." But the consequences did not disappear. The child died. The sword never departed from his house. David was forgiven, fully and completely, but he lived with the consequences of what he had done for the rest of his life. Psalm 51:3 — "My sin is ever before me." That is "How could you?" from the inside.

**Scripture:**
- Luke 15:20-32 (primary)
- 2 Samuel 12:13 — "The LORD also has taken away your sin; you shall not die." Forgiveness is real and complete — and consequences remain.
- Psalm 51:3 — "My sin is ever before me."
- 2 Corinthians 2:5-8 — Paul's instruction to forgive and comfort the repentant man "so that he will not be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow." There is a real danger of making restoration so punitive that it crushes the person it is supposed to restore.
- James 5:19-20 — "If any among you strays from the truth and one turns him back, let him know that he who turns a sinner from the error of his way will save his soul from death."

### Chapter 9 — The Long Road

**Theme:** Recovery is not a moment; it is a life. Relapse, trust rebuilt slowly, the daily choice.

**This is where most books on addiction stop being useful** — they treat the turning point as the ending. It is not. The turning point is the beginning of the hardest part.

**Freddie's second principle enters here:** Nancy Reagan promoted the slogan that people needed to "just say no" to drugs and alcohol. Freddie Anderson would say: "It isn't enough to just say no — you also have to get up and go!" Don't end up around that stuff to begin with. But if you do, remove yourself from the vicinity immediately. This is practical wisdom that applies not just to drugs and alcohol, but to every form of addiction and temptation.

**For the addict:** The daily discipline of sobriety. The triggers that don't go away. The relationships that need rebuilding one kept promise at a time. The temptation to think "I've got this" — which is often the moment before the fall. And the ongoing work of studying, learning, growing — Paul began attending every Bible study he could, and it was that daily discipline of being in the Word that sustained the change.

**"How could you?" lives here permanently.** This is the chapter where the book is honest about what the addict carries for the rest of his life. "How could you?" is a question that will haunt you. Not as punishment from God — the forgiveness is real. But as the weight of reality. David carried it: "My sin is ever before me." You will carry it. That is part of the cost of the choices you made. It is not a reason to stop walking the road. It is part of the road itself. And carrying it honestly, without hiding from it and without being crushed by it, is part of what the long road looks like.

**For the family:** Trust is rebuilt in inches, not miles. The difference between forgiveness (which can be immediate) and trust (which must be earned). How to hope without being naive. How to support without enabling.

**Honest handling of relapse:** Some people relapse. Some people relapse many times. The chapter addresses what happens when the turning doesn't stick — without offering false promises and without removing hope.

**Scripture:**
- Galatians 6:1 — "Brethren, even if anyone is caught in any trespass, you who are spiritual, restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness; each one looking to yourself, so that you too will not be tempted."
- Philippians 3:13-14 — "Forgetting what lies behind and reaching forward to what lies ahead, I press on toward the goal."
- 1 Corinthians 10:12 — "Therefore let him who thinks he stands take heed that he does not fall."
- 1 Corinthians 15:33 — "Do not be deceived: bad company corrupts good morals." (The associations must change — and stay changed.)

### Chapter 10 — The God Who Finds You

**Theme:** The gospel — for the reader (addict or family member) who realizes they need what this book has been describing.

**Primary text:** Acts 22:16 — "Now why do you delay? Get up and be baptized, and wash away your sins, calling on His name."

**Walk through the NT pattern:**
- Belief — John 8:24 ("Unless you believe that I am He, you will die in your sins")
- Repentance — Acts 2:38 ("Repent, and each of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins")
- Confession — Romans 10:9-10 ("If you confess with your mouth Jesus as Lord, and believe in your heart that God raised Him from the dead, you will be saved")
- Baptism — Romans 6:3-5 (buried with Him, raised to walk in newness of life), Acts 2:38, Acts 22:16

**NO thief-on-the-cross appeal** (Hebrews 9:16-17 — the will/testament was not in force while the testator lived).

**For the addict:** The emptiness that drove the addiction was a God-shaped hole filled with the wrong thing. The void is real. What you were looking for is real. You were just looking in the wrong place. Paul's own experience confirms this — he tried self-help books, he tried the wisdom of man, he tried to reason God away. None of it filled the void. Only the gospel did.

**For the family:** Some families will reach this chapter and realize that they have been going through this crisis without the foundation they need. The invitation is for them too.

**Paul's transformation:** Turning his life over to God and obeying the gospel made all the difference. Not a partial difference. Not an incremental improvement. All the difference. The man who walked into that prison at 17 is not the man writing this book at 65. The mind changed. The gaze was fixed. And everything followed.

**Tone:** Direct, urgent, honest. The reader has walked through nine chapters of honesty. This one doesn't flinch either.

## THEOLOGICAL GUARDRAILS

All guardrails from PRINCIPLES.md apply:

- Scripture interprets Scripture
- Biblical authority over human tradition
- Denominational neutrality — no denominational jargon, titles, or concepts
- CENI framework respected
- Old Testament / New Testament distinction maintained
- NASB preferred
- Honest handling of difficult passages
- No thief-on-the-cross template (Heb. 9:16-17)
- Do not apply male-specific NT roles/terms to women
- Where the text is silent, we are silent

### Additional Guardrails Specific to This Book

- **No clinical claims.** This is not a medical or psychological treatment book. It is a Scripture-grounded book that addresses the spiritual and relational dimensions of addiction. Do not diagnose, prescribe, or claim that faith replaces professional treatment.
- **No prosperity-gospel promises.** Do not promise that if you pray hard enough, your loved one will recover. Some don't. The book has to be honest about that.
- **No shame as a weapon.** The book will be honest — sometimes bluntly — but it will never use Scripture to beat someone who is already on the ground. Conviction is the Spirit's work; cruelty is not.
- **Addiction is not a single story.** Drugs, alcohol, pornography, gambling — the substances and behaviors vary, but the patterns are strikingly similar. The book should be broad enough to speak to all forms while being specific enough to feel real.
- **Personal responsibility without cruelty.** Ezekiel 18 must be handled with precision. The addict is responsible for their choices. The parents are not to blame for the child's decisions. But stating this truthfully is different from stating it coldly.
