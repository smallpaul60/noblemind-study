# Freddie Anderson — "Think on These Things"

## Lesson from Philippians 4:8

### Source: Summer series lecture (video transcript, curated for book reference)

---

## Context

Freddie Anderson delivered this lesson as part of a congregation's summer series. The subject assigned to him was "Think on These Things" from Philippians 4:8. Rather than limiting his treatment to that single verse, Freddie traced the apostle Paul's emphasis on the *mind* across his entire body of letters — arguing that "think" is the one reoccurring theme from Romans through Philemon, and that Paul's emphasis on the mind distinguishes him from every other New Testament writer.

This lesson provides the biblical framework for Chapter 6 of *Overcoming Addiction: Change the Mind, Change the Man* and informs the theological thesis of the entire book.

---

## Key Passages from the Lesson

### Paul's Unique Emphasis on the Mind

Freddie observed that the apostle Paul does something the other New Testament writers do not do. In all of his letters — to Rome, Galatia, Corinth, Philippi, Colossae, Thessalonica, and to the individuals Philemon, Timothy, and Titus — Paul emphasizes the *mind* more than Peter, James, John, Jude, or even the writer of Hebrews. Freddie attributed this in part to Paul's dual education — trained in the Jewish tradition under the most prominent scholar of his day, but also educated in the Hellenistic (Greek) world, knowing Greek law, customs, and language. God allowed Paul to integrate all of his experiences, along with the guidance of the Spirit, into his writings.

The word "mind" in Paul's usage could mean intent, attitude, will, or understanding. Freddie's central argument: Paul was telling them, over and over, across every letter — *think*.

### Don't Punctuate It — Think Goes Beyond Philippians 4:8

Freddie insisted that limiting "think on these things" to the punctuation of Philippians 4:8 does a disservice to the reader. The concept of *think* bleeds far beyond that single verse. Philippians 4:8 is Paul's doxology — his "finally, brothers and sisters" — the culmination of a theme he had been building across every letter he wrote. To drop anchor in that one verse and speak eloquently about its tenets, while ignoring the broader framework, would be to miss the point entirely.

> "If I were to just limit it to that eighth verse and where it is punctuated, then I would do you all a disservice as far as this subject is concerned. If I were to just leave it there with those words of that verse, then you all wouldn't get the richness that is really intended for that term or that thought — think on these things."

### Romans 12:1-2 — The Renewed Mind

Freddie connected "think" directly to Romans 12:1-2: "I beseech you by the mercies of God that you present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. And be not conformed to this world, but be ye transformed by the renewing of your *mind*." The transformation Paul calls for is a transformation of thinking.

### Philippians 2:5-8 — The Mind of Christ

On attitude: "Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus, who thought it not robbery to be equal with God, but made of himself no reputation, and was found in the form of a man, and he humbled himself even to the death of the cross." Freddie's point: *think* includes attitude — the kind of mind, the kind of thinking that characterized Christ.

### Philippians 2:2 — Spiritual Unity Is Unity of Mind

Freddie made a distinction between structural unity (Ephesians 4:1-5 — one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one hope, one God) and spiritual unity, which he located in Philippians 2:2: "Fulfill my joy, that you be like-minded, that you have the same love, that you be in one accord and of one mind." He unpacked each term:

- **Like-minded** — having the same priorities, the same focus. When you have the same focus, you'll have the same goals and aspirations.
- **One accord** — harmony. Not homogeneity (we don't all look alike), but the kind of harmony where we work together. He compared it to the concept of a musical chord — different parts, one sound.
- **Same love** — love without dissimulation (hypocrisy or partiality). James says we must not be respecters of persons. The way I love you, you ought to love me.
- **One mind** — the same kind of affection, the same kind of interest, focused similarly.

Freddie's conclusion: if elders wonder how to get everyone on the same page, the answer is right here — *think*.

### If They Would Just Think

Freddie argued that if a person were to genuinely reason — to sit down and honestly think — they could not help but come to the conclusions the Bible leads to. The problem is that people simply don't think.

> "Would you not agree, ladies and gentlemen, that if a person really, really thought about some things — one, they'd make a different decision; they wouldn't waste time the way they waste time; and they would do things differently. Think. Think. Think."

### The One Without-Fail Technique of Converting Somebody

Freddie posed the question: what is the one without-fail technique of converting somebody? His answer: getting them to *think*. He contrasted this with the typical approach — rushing in, flipping through Scripture rapidly — and argued instead for pausing, being quiet, and asking the person to think.

He illustrated with two examples:

1. At an evangelism workshop in Fort Worth, someone asked Freddie to visit with a man who was not a member of the body of Christ. It didn't take five minutes before the man had made a decision to obey the gospel. The only thing Freddie got him to do was *think*.

2. A mother called Freddie to come talk to her son Gus. Freddie asked Gus three questions: "Does Jesus want you to be saved?" (Yes.) "Gus, do you want to do what Jesus wants you to do?" (Yes.) "When do you think Jesus wants you to do it?" (Now.) Within minutes, Gus was being escorted to the church building for baptism. He was made to *think*.

### The Mind Changes Everything — The Prison Illustration

This is the passage most directly relevant to the book:

> "You can take a convict out of prison and put him in some brand new clothes and everything else and put him in the best, most perfect situation — and he'll go right back. But when you change his mind, it doesn't matter what degree of poverty he is in, he is not going to go back to the way that he was, or she was — because the mind is changed. Think."

This statement converges directly with Paul's (the author's) own experience: "Your circumstances have changed, but not your outlook. Not your thinking, and not your heart." It is the thesis of the book expressed in two voices — the teacher's and the student's.

### What Paul Kept Saying

Freddie's summary: from Romans chapter 1 all the way to Philemon, the one reoccurring theme is *think*. The mind. Over and over and over again. Because Paul the apostle knew that if you could change a person's mind, you could change everything about them. And if you don't change their mind, you don't change anything about them.

### Think on These Things

Freddie concluded by returning to Philippians 4:8, but now with the full weight of context behind it:

> "Think on these things. What things to think about? Think about the love of God. The sacrifice of Jesus. Think about heaven's hope. Think about those things. I guarantee you, ladies and gentlemen, I guarantee you that when we think on those things, it will stabilize our hearts. It'll stabilize our faith. It will give us the energy that we need to finish the race. Think on eternal life. Think on these things."

## Application to the Book

### Chapter 4 — "All of the Imprisoned Aren't in Prison"
Freddie's observation (from elsewhere in his teaching, not this specific transcript) opens this chapter and broadens the audience.

### Chapter 6 — "Think"
This lesson is the foundation of the hinge chapter. The key elements:
- Paul the apostle's unique emphasis on the mind across every letter
- The concept that "think" encompasses intent, attitude, will, and understanding
- Romans 12:2 — transformation by the renewing of the mind
- Philippians 2:5 — the mind of Christ
- The prison illustration — change the mind, change the man
- The evangelism method — get them to think

### Chapter 9 — "The Long Road"
Freddie's principle (from separate teaching): "It isn't enough to just say no — you also have to get up and go!" This applies to the daily discipline of recovery and the practical wisdom of removing yourself from temptation.

### Book Thesis
Freddie's central argument — that if you change a person's mind, you change everything about them, and if you don't, you change nothing — is the thesis of the book stated in its simplest form. The subtitle, *Change the Mind, Change the Man*, is drawn directly from this teaching.

## Note on Usage

Freddie Anderson is credited by name in the text of the book, not merely in acknowledgments. His teaching is adapted and woven into the narrative with direct attribution, not block-quoted. The author (Paul) identifies Freddie as the preacher who had the most profound impact on his life and his approach to Scripture — the man who always said, "That's a good question — let's see what the Bible says."
