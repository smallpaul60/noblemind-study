# Panel 8 — Love does not take into account a wrong suffered

**Concept:** An after-school classroom. Two teen boys are side by side at a worktable, leaning over a large hand-drawn poster project they are finishing together. The subject — a Korean-American teen boy — is mid-reach, passing a marker across to his friend without looking up from his own work. The friend — a white teen boy — is taking the marker with a small grateful glance toward the subject. Their shoulders are close, their hands share the workspace, and there is no division anywhere in the body language. Yesterday they had a fight. Today they are heads-down on this together.

**Why this composition:** The Greek *ou logizetai to kakon* — "does not reckon the evil" — is an accounting metaphor. Love doesn't keep a ledger. The hardest thing about this verse for an image is that the wrong has *already happened*; we have to picture an absence of grudge. The composition handles this by showing the *aftermath stage* — post-conflict, post-cooldown — and making the visible cue **shared workspace with no spatial guarding**. Their elbows almost touch. The marker passes hand-to-hand across the poster. The subject has no body-tension that says "I am still keeping track of yesterday." Per [[smallest-physical-fix-first]], the verse is in the geometry of their two bodies sharing one table — not in any expression-acrobatics.

**Why the friend's micro-grateful glance:** Without a tiny narrative cue from the friend, the panel reads as just "two friends doing homework." The friend's brief upward look as he takes the marker — a small flicker of relief that the subject came back and is working alongside him as if nothing happened — is the narrative tell. The subject's calm un-look-up is the verse: he is not extracting anything from the friend, not making him grovel, not even acknowledging that he is forgiving him. He is just working on the poster.

**Why same gender:** Panel 8 is about post-conflict forgiveness, and a cross-race pairing here risks viewers reading the conflict as racially loaded ("subject forgiving the other race"), which would hijack the verse. Two boys of different ethnicities working as peer friends is exactly the everyday picture — a fight between best friends, now resolved.

## Note before you generate

Four specific failure modes to watch for:

1. **It reads as just "two friends doing homework."** Without the friend's small grateful glance, the verse is invisible — the panel becomes generic. The flicker in his eyes as he takes the marker is the entire narrative tell. Regen note: `as the friend takes the marker, his eyes briefly flick up toward the subject with a small grateful expression — a quiet "thanks, we're good." Mouth in a soft almost-smile. Not staged, not big — just a flicker of relief.`

2. **One boy is doing everything; the other is watching.** If one is hanging back while the other works, the panel reads "the offender is making it up to him" — too transactional. Both must be visibly contributing in equal measure. Regen note: `both boys are actively working — one mid-reach passing a marker, the other receiving it and ready to use it. Both have hands on the poster. Equal participation.`

3. **The subject looks performatively gracious.** Bright forgiving smile aimed at the friend = the wrong cue. The subject must look absorbed in the work, not performing graciousness. Regen note: `the subject's eyes are on his own section of the poster, not on his friend. His expression is calm and absorbed — the quiet focus of someone in the middle of a task, not the staged warmth of someone "being nice."`

4. **They are angled away from each other / spatially guarded.** If their bodies twist away, if there is air between them, if hands are in pockets — the verse collapses. Regen note: `their shoulders are very close, almost touching. Elbows nearly touching. Both leaning into the same shared workspace. No spatial guarding. No arms crossed. No hands in pockets.`
