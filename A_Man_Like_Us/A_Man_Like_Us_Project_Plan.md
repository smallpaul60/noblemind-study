# A Man Like Us
## What Scripture Says When You Are Weary

**Project planning document — Noble Mind**
**Status:** Future project. Bones captured for development when the time is right.

## Working Title

**A Man Like Us: What Scripture Says When You Are Weary**

The title alone is evocative but ambiguous; the subtitle does critical clarifying work, telling the tired reader exactly what they are holding — a Scripture-anchored book about weariness. The full title is the form to use on the cover.

A market check confirmed the title itself is available — the phrase appears frequently in sermons and blog posts because it is taken from James 5:17, but no published book uses it as its title.

## Anchor Verse

> *"Elijah was a man with a nature like ours, and he prayed earnestly that it might not rain, and it did not rain on the earth for three years and six months."* — **James 5:17**

This verse is the inspired commentary that justifies the entire book. The Holy Spirit, through James, tells us that the great figures of Scripture had natures *like ours.* The book applies this principle across the whole canon — the faithful of Scripture grew weary, and Scripture preserved their weariness on purpose.

## Audience

Believers who already trust God. Not in a faith crisis. Not seeking evangelism. Not looking for self-help.

They are simply tired.

This is a pastoral book, not an evangelistic one. The reader does not need to be convinced of the gospel — the reader needs to be reminded that the faithful have always grown weary, and that God has always met them in it.

## Thesis

The faithful of Scripture grew weary. Scripture preserved their weariness on purpose. God met every one of them in it. He will meet the reader, too.

The book is not a book of solutions. It is a book of pictures — the picture of God meeting His servants in their weariness, over and over, throughout the entire arc of Scripture, ending in Christ Himself.

## Provisional Chapter List

1. **Elijah under the juniper tree** — the weariness of feeling alone (1 Kings 19, anchored in James 5:17)
2. **Moses, ready to quit** — the weariness of carrying too much (Numbers 11)
3. **David in the cave** — the weariness of being hunted (Psalm 55, 57, 142)
4. **Job in the ashes** — the weariness of unexplained suffering (Job 3, anchored in James 5:11)
5. **Jeremiah, the weeping prophet** — the weariness of speaking truth no one wants to hear (Jeremiah 20:9)
6. **Jonah under the plant** — the weariness of disagreeing with God (Jonah 4)
7. **Habakkuk on the watchtower** — the weariness of unanswered questions (Habakkuk 3:17–19)
8. **John the Baptist in prison** — the weariness of the cell after the calling (Matthew 11:3)
9. **Paul with the thorn** — the weariness of unanswered prayer for relief (2 Corinthians 12)
10. **Jesus in Gethsemane** — the weariness of the cup (Matthew 26, anchored in Hebrews 4:15)
11. **The reader's chapter** — what these stories say to you

The Christ chapter near the end is critical. It is the deepest comfort Scripture offers — not just that God *understands* our weariness, but that the Son of God *experienced* it. The title earns its full weight there: the One who had a nature *unlike* ours became a man *like* us, so that He could meet us in our weariness with sympathy (Hebrews 4:15).

## Market Context

A market check before development confirmed that books on biblical figures and weariness are well-occupied territory. Specifically:

**Jill Briscoe — *Running on Empty*** is the closest direct competitor. The reviewer summary describes it as "a book that lets you go dive in to the lives of Biblical personalities and how God dealt with them in their predicaments... You might find the usual characters like Elijah, David, Moses and Jonah but added some interesting figures like Leah, Naomi and Habakkuk." That overlap with the proposed chapter list is significant — Elijah, David, Moses, Jonah, and Habakkuk are all in common.

**Tish Harrison Warren — *What Grows in Weary Lands*** (recent release) targets the same emotional terrain — burnout, weariness, spiritual aridity — drawing on early Christian monastic tradition and personal reflection.

**Sarah J. Hauser — *All Who Are Weary*** uses the Matthew 11:28 framing, organized around nine "soul-crushing burdens."

**Article-level coverage is heavy.** Multiple articles list Elijah, Moses, David, Jonah, and Job as Bible characters who experienced weariness or depression. The connection of these figures to weariness is well-established in Christian writing.

The premise has been done. The angles have been worked. *Another* book with the same characters in a similar treatment will struggle to differentiate itself unless the distinctive voice and discipline are sharp enough to make it a different kind of book.

That sharpening — what makes *this* book different — is the critical work below.

## Distinctives — what this book must do that the existing ones do not

These are the load-bearing differentiators. If the book is not visibly doing these things, it will be redundant. If it is, it will be its own thing — and people who trust the Noble Mind voice will want to read it.

### 1. Scripture interpreting Scripture, applied to character study

Most existing weariness books draw devotional or psychological lessons from biblical figures. Briscoe writes as a devotional teacher. Warren draws on monastic tradition and her own experience of burnout. Almost none rigorously anchor their treatment of Old Testament figures in the New Testament's own commentary on those figures.

This book will do that as a discipline. James 5:17 on Elijah. James 5:11 on Job. Hebrews 11 on Moses, David, and the cumulative witnesses of faith. Romans 15:4 — *"For whatever was written in earlier times was written for our instruction."* 1 Corinthians 10:11 — *"Now these things happened to them as an example, and they were written for our instruction."* When the Spirit gives us inspired commentary on an Old Testament figure, we use it. We let the New Testament tell us how to read the Old.

This is not a small thing. It is the difference between *the author has thoughts about Elijah* and *here is how the apostle James told us to read Elijah.* The reader feels that difference, even if they cannot name it.

### 2. The Christ chapter as the structural anchor

Most weariness books reference Jesus but do not build toward Him as the climactic answer. The book becomes a series of devotional case studies, with Christ as one example among others.

This book treats Gethsemane as the load-bearing wall of the entire structure. Every prior chapter is pointing forward to *the One who became a man like us, so He could meet us in our weariness.* Every Old Testament figure is a witness. Every weariness they bore is a shadow of the weariness He bore. The title *A Man Like Us* earns its full weight there: the One whose nature was *unlike* ours became a man *like* us, so He could be a high priest who sympathizes with our weakness (Hebrews 4:15).

That Christological spine distinguishes the book from a devotional collection. It is not a tour of biblical figures who got tired. It is a long, deliberate movement toward Gethsemane, where the comfort is finally complete.

### 3. The diagnostic framing — what kind of weariness are you in?

Most existing books use these figures to teach general lessons about endurance, perseverance, or trust. The treatment is largely interchangeable from chapter to chapter.

This book takes a different approach: each chapter names a *distinct kind of weariness*, and shows how God's response to that specific kind was different.

- The weariness of feeling alone is not the weariness of carrying too much.
- The weariness of being hunted is not the weariness of unexplained suffering.
- The weariness of speaking truth no one wants to hear is not the weariness of disagreeing with God.
- The weariness of unanswered questions is not the weariness of the cell after the calling.
- The weariness of unanswered prayer for relief is not the weariness of the cup.

The reader is invited to recognize *which* kind they are currently in — because the comfort changes depending on the diagnosis. God did not respond to Elijah the way He responded to Job. He did not answer Habakkuk the way He answered Jonah. This is a book that takes the texture of each weariness seriously, and trusts the reader to find their own kind among the witnesses.

### 4. The Noble Mind voice and theological framework

Churches of Christ theological framing is a smaller niche than the broader evangelical market most of these books operate in, and within that niche there are very few books on this subject. The discipline of New Testament authority, silence of Scripture as non-authorization, and no denominational language — these are part of the voice readers of *From the Beginning* will recognize and trust.

The book will read like Noble Mind. That alone makes it different.

### 5. The voice itself

Tired readers do not want to be shouted at. They do not want to be jollied along. They do not want a teacher correcting them. They want a faithful friend opening Scripture beside them. The book should feel pastoral, patient, unhurried — closer to the dedication of *From the Beginning* than to the urgent passages of the response chapters.

The ambient voice in this category often drifts toward sentimentality, optimism, or a kind of practiced empathy that does not actually meet the reader where they are. The cure is staying anchored in the text. Every comfort the book offers must be a comfort Scripture itself offers. If we cannot find it in the text, we do not say it.

## Discipline Notes

These are the principles that should guide the writing, drawn from how the Noble Mind voice has developed across previous projects.

**NASB throughout.** The primary translation, consistent with all other Noble Mind work. Other translations may be referenced for comparison or clarity, but NASB is the default.

**Anchor each chapter in New Testament commentary on the Old Testament figure where one exists.** James 5:17 on Elijah. James 5:11 on Job. Hebrews 11 on Moses, David, and others. This is the discipline of letting Scripture interpret Scripture, applied to character study.

**Resist over-application.** A common failure of devotional books is squeezing every story for three points and an action plan. Scripture does not always give us application. Sometimes Scripture just gives us the *picture* of God meeting His servant in weariness — and the comfort is in the picture itself, not in extracting a how-to. Trust the readers. Let the story do its own work.

**Honor the distinct kind of weariness in each story.** Each chapter is its own diagnosis. Do not let the chapters blur into one another.

**Christ chapter anchors everything.** The verse that closes the chapter (and arguably the book): *"For we do not have a high priest who cannot sympathize with our weaknesses, but One who has been tempted in all things as we are, yet without sin."* — Hebrews 4:15.

**Quiet voice, not performative.** Pastoral. Patient. Unhurried.

**Avoid sentimentality.** Stay anchored in the text. Every comfort must be a comfort Scripture itself offers.

**Stay within the Churches of Christ framework.** Same theological guardrails as all Noble Mind work. New Testament authority. Silence of Scripture as non-authorization. No denominational language. No tradition imposed on top of the text.

## Pre-Development Reading

Before drafting begins, we should read at least two of the existing books in the space to understand what is already being said and to be sure we are not unconsciously repeating what is already in print:

1. **Jill Briscoe — *Running on Empty.*** The closest competitor; required reading. Particularly important to understand how she treats Elijah, David, Moses, Jonah, and Habakkuk so we can see clearly where our angle differs.

2. **Tish Harrison Warren — *What Grows in Weary Lands.*** Recent and well-positioned in the audience space. Warren writes from a different theological tradition; understanding her angle helps us know what the broader market is currently being told.

A short note for each book — what it does well, where its angle differs from ours, what we should avoid duplicating — would be useful to file alongside this plan before any chapter is drafted.

## Connection to the Broader Noble Mind Project

*A Man Like Us* would reach a different audience than *From the Beginning.*

*From the Beginning* is for someone with little or no biblical foundation — adults who have drifted from faith and their children. It walks a reader from creation through resurrection.

*A Man Like Us* is for someone who already believes — and is tired. That is a huge audience, and one the church often fails to serve well. It can sit beside *From the Beginning* on the same shelf, in the same voice, under the same brand, but reaching a reader at a different moment in their walk.

It can also stand independent of the timeline study (*The Last Week of the Lamb*) and the baptism booklet (*Is Baptism Really Necessary?*). It does not depend on any of those projects to make sense.
