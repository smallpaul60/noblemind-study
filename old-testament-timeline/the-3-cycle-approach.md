# Outline for Classes — The Three Cycle Approach

**by Bob Waldron**
**Guardian of Truth Foundation, 2005 · ISBN 1-58427-127-2**

Reference framework for granular Old Testament (and Bible-wide) timeline
events. Captured from the scanned booklet on 2026-05-26 for use as the
event-level companion to the macro-narrative captured in
[unfolding-of-gods-plan.md](unfolding-of-gods-plan.md).

---

## How to Use This Information

Waldron opens with a candid acknowledgment: extensive studies like this one
are hard for someone who has not already done the background work. Two
audiences he writes for:

1. Someone who already has an extensive Bible knowledge and wants this
   organized to teach it to others, or to prepare himself for future Bible
   study.
2. Someone who *does not* have this knowledge, who will eventually teach
   the material themselves.

The format follows the way Waldron actually teaches it — including the
techniques he uses. The student is invited to ignore any of his suggestions
and follow what works for him personally.

One suggestion: don't read straight through. Study the first cycle, learn it,
recite it back, *then* move to the second cycle. By the third cycle the
student should be quite comfortable with the earlier information.

---

## Objectives

Three objectives drive the whole method:

1. **Etch the basic facts of Bible history into the student's mind.** Effective
   repetition is more helpful than prolonged exposure. Learning done in
   *cycles* that are repeated provides three cycles of repetition.
2. **Provide better access to the knowledge gained from Bible study.** Through
   organization, the student remembers facts as nearly permanent ones — and
   helps them link new facts into the network they already have.
3. **See that the Bible records the fulfillment of a plan, God's plan for the
   ages.** This study takes the student *through the Bible three times*, and
   it does it in such a way that the student has to look at the Bible story
   as a whole.

## The Vase-in-the-Warehouse Analogy (Waldron's central illustration)

> "So often we learn facts that are isolated. They are never integrated
> into our 'web' or network of Bible knowledge. The result is that we
> tend to forget the fact and have no idea where it is. Let me use two
> illustrations to show how the approach in this material helps solve
> this problem. One involves an enormous warehouse. Someone brings a
> vase to be stored. We put the vase in an unmarked box. We put that
> box into a much larger box that contains lots of other boxes. This
> box is also unmarked. Then we take the whole box on a forklift back
> to the innards of the warehouse and stack it on other boxes just
> like it. The warehouse is not divided into sections, or rows, or
> anything. Six months later, the person comes back and asks for his
> vase. We have no memory that there was a vase, and we certainly have
> no idea where it is. If we had marked the box with the vase, and had
> divided the warehouse into sections, rows, shelves, etc., we could
> go right to the vase."

The cycles build that warehouse organization in the mind. Isolated facts
become networked associations: the more threads attached to a fact, the
less fragile its place in memory. **The more associations we have with a
fact, the more securely that fact will be in our mind.**

He notes the implication: this also explains why it is easier to learn a
fact in a subject about which one already knows something. When one has a
network of knowledge already established, it is simple to incorporate
another fact into it.

## The Three Cycles

### Cycle 1 — Periods of Bible History (the framework)

**Primary objective:** Learn the list of periods of Bible history and how
they link together. Not just memorize them — see how they are related,
how one leads to the next.

Topics in this cycle are mostly *periods* and *events* of the Bible. The
point is not that the Bible is not just historical material; it also
contains doctrinal material such as the epistles. The first-cycle goal is
to create a directory, a pigeonhole for every fact in the Bible.

Waldron defends the granularity explicitly: better too many periods than
too few. If there are too few, students have to find too many of the
events to fill them up — a "fine line between periods" they cannot draw.
He chose **79 periods** (events distributed across the major epochs) as
the right balance.

### Cycle 2 — Names of Periods Backwards (verifying the network)

Once cycle one is complete, the second cycle takes the student through
the periods *backwards* — naming them along a little by asking the right
question. If the network is real, the student will be able to do this
remarkably well. If you are studying this material for yourself, you will
be able to do so also.

### Cycle 3 — Scriptural Limits of Each Period (drilling the references)

Once the second cycle is finished, the third cycle reinforces the list of
periods and categories backwards. The goal: identify the Scriptural limits
of each period — chapter and verse — so each period is firmly tied to its
text. The student begins to be able to do the linking work himself: "this
event is in this period, which is in this section, which is part of God's
unfolding plan."

## The Periods (Cycle 1 — the framework that drives the timeline)

The following are Waldron's named periods and sub-events. Together they
total roughly 79 distinguishable items, distributed across major epochs.

### 1. Before the Flood — Genesis 1–5
- Creation of the world (Gen 1)
- Adam and Eve in the Garden of Eden (Gen 2)
- First sin (Gen 3)
- Cain and Abel (Gen 4)
- Generations of Adam (Gen 5)
- Fast-forwards to the time of the Flood

### 2. The Flood — Genesis 6–10
- Chapters 6–8: the flood itself
- Meat given for food
- Law of capital punishment
- Rainbow covenant
- Curse on Canaan
- Chapter 10: Generations of Noah's sons
- Fast-forwards to time of Tower of Babel

### 3. Scattering of the People — Genesis 11
- Tower of Babel built
- Languages divided
- Generations: transition to patriarchs

### 4. The Patriarchs — Genesis 12–50
- Chapters 12–25: Abraham
- Chapters 25–28: Isaac
- Chapters 28–35: Jacob
- Chapters 37–50: Joseph (transition to the period of the Exodus)

### 5. The Exodus — Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers 1–13
- Israel in bondage
- Moses' birth
- Raised as son of Pharaoh's daughter
- Rejected by his people
- Flees to wilderness
- Burning bush
- Plagues
- Crossing of the Red Sea
- Trip to Sinai
- Events at Mount Sinai
- Trip to Kadesh
- Spies sent into the land
- Israel refuses to go in

### 6. Wandering in the Wilderness — Numbers 14–36, Deuteronomy, Joshua 1–5
- Israel sentenced to wander in the wilderness
- Korah, Dathan, Abiram
- Aaron's rod that budded
- Miriam dies
- Sin of Moses and Aaron
- Passing by Edom
- Aaron dies
- Fiery serpents
- Conquer Sihon (Amorites)
- Conquer Og (Bashan)
- Balaam
- Fornication with Midianites
- Second census
- Slaughter of Midianites
- Speeches of Moses in Deuteronomy

### 7. Invasion and Conquest — Joshua 4–24
- Crossing of the Jordan
- Observance of the Passover
- Chapters 4–8: Central campaign
- Chapters 9–10: Southern campaign
- Chapter 11: Northern campaign
- Chapter 12: List of kings conquered
- Chapters 13–19: Land divided among the tribes
- Chapters 20–21: Cities of refuge and other cities given to the priests and Levites
- Chapter 22: Transjordanic tribes return home
- Chapter 23: Joshua challenges Israel to forsake idols and remain faithful to Jehovah
- Chapter 24: Covenant renewed at Shechem

### 8. The Judges — Judges, Ruth, 1 Samuel 1–9
The first thirteen judges, plus Eli and Samuel:
- Othniel
- Ehud
- Shamgar
- Deborah (with Barak)
- Gideon
- Abimelech (not a judge, but a leader)
- Tola
- Jair
- Jephthah
- Ibzan
- Elon
- Abdon
- Samson
- Eli
- Samuel
- Story of Micah
- Levite's concubine
- Book of Ruth (fits into this period)

### 9. United Kingdom — 1 Samuel 10 – 1 Kings 11
- Saul (1 Samuel 10–31)
- David (2 Samuel; 1 Chronicles 10–29)
- Solomon (1 Kings 1–11; 2 Chronicles 1–9)

Note from Waldron: the Chronicles parallel begins in 1 Chronicles 10 with
David, omitting Saul almost entirely (the main difficulty: that the two
narratives don't align period-by-period).

### 10. Divided Kingdom — 1 Kings 12 – 2 Kings 17
- Kingdom of Israel in the north
- Kingdom of Judah in the south
- The Divided Kingdom is not as clearly set forth in 2 Chronicles as it is in
  1 Kings 12 – 2 Kings 17
- Prophets active in this period: Obadiah, Joel, Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah
- Isaiah continues into the time of Judah alone

### 11. Judah Alone — 2 Kings 18–25, 2 Chronicles 29–36
After the fall of Israel (722 BC), Judah continues alone. Six chapters in
2 Kings; eight in 2 Chronicles.
- Kings: Hezekiah, Manasseh, Josiah
- Prophets: Zephaniah, Jeremiah, Habakkuk, Nahum

### 12. Captivity — Daniel, Ezekiel (plus references in Jeremiah, Lamentations)
- Daniel (in the courts of Nebuchadnezzar and Darius)
- Ezekiel (among the common people in exile)

### 13. Return — Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi
- Ezra 1–6: First return under Zerubbabel (536 BC)
- Books of Haggai and Zechariah (prophesying during the temple rebuild)
- Book of Esther (the story of Esther occurs between Ezra 6 and 7)
- Ezra 7–10: Ezra leads a return (458 BC)
- Book of Nehemiah (the walls rebuilt in 52 days)
- Book of Malachi (the last OT prophet)

### 14. Years of Silence — No Scripture
The roughly 400 years between the close of the Old Testament and the New.
Greek then Roman dominance; the synagogue system; the Septuagint; rise of
the Pharisees, Sadducees, and other parties.

### 15. Life of Christ — Matthew, Mark, Luke, John
Seven sub-periods Waldron drills explicitly:
- Years of Preparation
- Beginning of His ministry
- Great Galilean Ministry
- Periods of Retirement
- Closing of His ministry
- Last Week
- Resurrection Days

### 16. Early Church — Acts
A span of about 30 years total.
- Acts 1–12: Primarily the work in Jerusalem, Palestine, and the eastern end
  of the Mediterranean (covers about 15 years)
- Acts 13–28: Primarily the work of Paul

### 17. Letters to Christians — The Epistles
Doctrinal rather than historical material. Use the chapter divisions of
Acts to place these letters in time relative to where they were written:
- 1 and 2 Thessalonians
- Galatians
- 1 and 2 Corinthians
- Romans
- The Prison Epistles, Pastoral Epistles, General Epistles, Revelation
  (covered by the next-cycle drill on the "fourth cycle" of doctrinal study)

## Fourth Cycle — A Lifetime of Study

Once the framework of periods is etched in memory, Waldron offers a
*fourth* (and ongoing) cycle: the lifelong study where every new fact
finds its home in the warehouse. The first three cycles build the
warehouse; the fourth cycle fills it. **The basic relationship God wants
with men is best built upon a sound knowledge of the Bible itself.**

## Structural Notes for the Timeline

### What this gives us that the *Unfolding* booklet didn't

The *Unfolding of God's Plan* booklet gives the narrative arc (18 sections,
"phases" in our timeline language). This booklet gives the **granular
event list** under each phase. They are not competing — they are *the
same framework at two levels of zoom*:

| Unfolding (macro-phase) | 3-Cycle (granular period) |
|---|---|
| The Creation | Before the Flood |
| The first promise of a Savior | (within Before the Flood — Gen 3) |
| The Flood | The Flood |
| Promise to Abraham: Nation, Land, Spiritual | The Patriarchs |
| Jacob's Twelve Sons | The Patriarchs (continued) |
| The Family in Egypt | (within The Exodus — early chapters) |
| The Exodus From Egypt | The Exodus |
| People forced to wander in the wilderness | Wandering in the Wilderness |
| Invasion and conquest of the land | Invasion and Conquest |
| The Judges | The Judges |
| The United Kingdom | United Kingdom |
| The Fall of Israel | Divided Kingdom (closes with Israel's fall, 722 BC) |
| The Fall of Judah | Judah Alone (closes with Judah's fall, 586 BC) |
| The Return | Return |
| The Years of Silence | Years of Silence |
| The Birth of Jesus | Life of Christ (Years of Preparation, etc.) |
| Go tell the good news! | Early Church |
| The Perfect Law of Liberty | Letters to Christians |

The two booklets are the macro and the micro of the same study. Waldron
uses them together pedagogically. We can use them together architecturally.

### Mapping to the noblemind.study timeline architecture

We can use the *exact* same PHASES + events pattern we used for Paul.
With Waldron's framework:

- **PHASES** = the 17 major periods (one phase per Waldron period, OT through Letters)
- **EVENTS** = the ~79 sub-items (Waldron's bullet points under each period)
- **DEEPDIVE SPOKES** = optional sub-timelines for periods that justify it
  (e.g., Life of Christ has its own 7-part sub-structure; Letters to
  Christians could be a sub-spoke; the Patriarchs could be split into
  Abraham/Isaac/Jacob/Joseph sub-spokes)

### Where the granularity lands

The OT-only portion (phases 1–13 above) gives us roughly:
- **5 + 6 + 3 + 4 + 13 + 15 + 12 + 18 + 3 + ~10 + ~5 + ~5 + ~10 = ~109 events**

That's larger than the Paul Life timeline (which is ~86 events), but
manageable. If we limit ourselves to OT-only (phases 1–13), the timeline
covers from Creation to Malachi. If we extend to phases 14–17, we get the
whole Bible.

**Recommendation for the first build**: OT only (phases 1–13). The NT
side is already substantially covered by the Apostle Paul hub and the
existing Acts-Enhanced lessons. Building the OT side completes the
project's coverage; tacking the NT on top of Waldron's framework would
mostly duplicate what we have.

### Pedagogical implications for the design

Because Waldron's whole point is the *network* — facts gain power from
their associations — the timeline should make those associations
visible. Concrete UI ideas:

- **Each event card** should name its period explicitly (the "phase" we
  already render does this).
- **Cross-references** (e.g., the story of Ruth fits in the Judges
  period, but Ruth → Obed → Jesse → David is a thread reaching forward;
  Joseph's birth in the Patriarchs period reaches forward to The Exodus)
  — these could appear as small "see also" links on the relevant cards.
- **The threefold promise to Abraham** could be tracked across the
  timeline as a sub-thread visualization — when a promise gets
  fulfilled, the relevant thread gets "closed."
- **The royal line** (David → through exile → return) is another
  long-running thread.

These threads are not events themselves but *trajectories through
events*. Waldron's whole pedagogy says they should be made visible.
