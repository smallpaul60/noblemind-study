# Principles of The Noble Mind Study Tool

> *"Now these were more noble-minded than those in Thessalonica, for they received the word with great eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so."*  
> — Acts 17:11 (NASB)

---

## The Name

The name "Noble Mind" comes from the Bereans described in Acts 17. When Paul preached to them, they didn't simply accept or reject his teaching based on preference, tradition, or the authority of the speaker. Instead, they **received the word with eagerness** and then **examined the Scriptures daily** to verify whether what they were being taught was actually true.

This is the spirit this tool is designed to serve: eager reception of God's word combined with careful, daily examination of Scripture.

---

## Foundational Principles

### 1. Scripture Interprets Scripture

The Bible is its own best interpreter. When seeking to understand any passage, we look to:

1. **Immediate context** — the verses, paragraphs, and chapters surrounding the text
2. **Cross-references** — other passages that address the same topic or use the same language
3. **The broader testimony of Scripture** — the unified message of the whole Bible

We do not impose meanings from outside Scripture. We let the Bible define its own terms and explain its own concepts.

All cross-references and interpretive connections must come from Scripture itself. Do not import conclusions from commentaries, systematic theologies, or scholarly consensus. When the Bible explains itself (e.g., Hebrews 11:17–19 explaining Abraham's reasoning in Genesis 22), say so explicitly: "This is not commentary. This is Scripture interpreting Scripture."

### 2. Biblical Authority Over Human Tradition

Man is fallible; God's word is not.

When there is a conflict between what Scripture says and what religious tradition teaches, Scripture takes precedence. The Bereans were praised precisely because they tested even an apostle's teaching against the written word. We should do no less with any human teacher, commentary, creed, or tradition.

*"But in vain do they worship Me, teaching as doctrines the precepts of men."* — Matthew 15:9

### Denominational Neutrality

All content must be free of denominational terminology, titles, and concepts. When working from source material — especially rewrites of older denominational works — we must carefully remove any language that reflects a particular denomination's traditions rather than Scripture itself.

This includes:
- **Denominational titles:** Use "preacher" or "evangelist" rather than "pastor," "bishop," "reverend," or "father" when referring to those who preach the gospel (these terms carry denominational baggage that differs from their biblical usage)
- **Denominational concepts:** Do not import organizational structures, practices, or assumptions that belong to a particular denomination rather than to the New Testament pattern
- **Unwarranted inferences about the text:** Do not add descriptors or characterizations that go beyond what Scripture actually states (e.g., calling someone "wealthy" when the text only says they "contributed from their own means")
- **Denominational lenses:** No Reformed lens, no charismatic lens, no Catholic lens. Just the text, the context, the cross-references.
- **The word "church":** The word "church" (ekklesia) in Scripture refers to the body of Christ, not a building. Do not use it to mean a building or a gathering place. Use "room," "assembly," or other appropriate terms when referring to a physical space.

The goal is to let Scripture speak for itself without any denominational filter.

### 3. The CENI Framework

Sound biblical interpretation recognizes that God communicates His will through:

- **Commands** — Direct instructions from God
- **Examples** — Approved patterns of action in Scripture
- **Necessary Inferences** — Conclusions that must logically follow from what Scripture teaches

This framework helps distinguish between what God has authorized and what is merely human preference or invention.

### 4. Rightly Dividing the Word

*"Be diligent to present yourself approved to God as a workman who does not need to be ashamed, accurately handling the word of truth."* — 2 Timothy 2:15

We recognize the distinction between:

- **The Old Testament** (Law of Moses) — given to Israel, fulfilled in Christ
- **The New Testament** (Law of Christ) — the authority for the church today

While all Scripture is profitable for teaching (2 Timothy 3:16-17), Christians live under Christ's law, not Moses' law (Galatians 6:2, Hebrews 8:13).

### 5. Logic and Evidence Over Consensus

Truth is not determined by majority vote or popular opinion. We follow the evidence of Scripture wherever it leads, even when that contradicts widely-held assumptions.

This applies to:
- Religious questions (testing all teaching against Scripture)
- Origins questions (examining assumptions, following observational evidence)
- Any truth claim (testing it by sound logic and available evidence)

*"Test all things; hold fast what is good."* — 1 Thessalonians 5:21

## Honest Handling of Scripture

### Let the Text Speak

Ask "What does the text actually say?" before asking "What does it mean?" When the text says something interesting, stop and notice it. Do not rush past details. But do not build doctrines on observations the text does not explain. Present what the text says, ask the reader to consider it, and trust them.

### Do Not Claim What the Text Does Not Say

Even if a statement sounds powerful or poetic, do not present it as fact if the text does not support it. If the text is silent on a point, we are silent on it — or we say plainly that the text does not tell us.

### Acknowledge Difficulty and Disagreement

When a passage is genuinely difficult or when faithful students have disagreed, say so. Never twist Scripture to fit a predetermined conclusion. Be willing to say "I don't know" when the text does not provide a clear answer. Present what the text says, even when it challenges popular assumptions.

### Present Observations Without Pushing Conclusions

When Scripture preserves a detail, a distinction, or a thread across passages, present the facts, show the cross-references, and let the reader trace the thread. Do not use leading questions or rhetorical framing that steers the reader toward a conclusion the text does not explicitly draw. Trust the reader to see what the text shows them.

### Internal Consistency in Claims

If you establish that a person did NOT do something (e.g., Hagar was not crying out to God), do not later phrase something in a way that implies they did. Readers — and especially critical readers — will catch the contradiction. Every claim must be consistent with every other claim in the same work.

---

## No Man-Made Labels

### Doctrinal Labels

Do NOT use terms like "Trinity," "Tetragrammaton," or other labels that men invented to describe biblical concepts. Let Scripture reveal what it reveals. If the text shows the Father, the Son, and the Spirit acting as one God, say that — but use the language of Scripture, not the language of creeds. The text is sufficient.

### Scholarly Jargon

Describe what the Hebrew or Greek says; do not label it with scholarly jargon the reader will not know and the Bible never uses. Explain the language when it matters. Never explain it for show.

### Etymology from Usage, Not Speculation

When a Hebrew word's root is debated (as with Shaddai), do not pick a root and present it as settled. Instead, let the usage pattern across Scripture define the meaning. What the text consistently shows is more reliable than what linguists argue about.

---

## Preferred Study Text

While this tool supports multiple Bible translations, the **New American Standard Bible (NASB)** is the preferred text for study due to its commitment to formal equivalence (word-for-word translation philosophy) and its accuracy in rendering the original languages. Reference other translations when helpful for comparison or clarity. When discussing translation choices, explain the underlying Greek or Hebrew when relevant.

---

## For Origins and Science Questions

Follow strict logic and observational evidence rather than defaulting to consensus. Examine the assumptions behind claims. Recognize that consensus measures agreement among people, not truth. History shows that scientific consensus has often been wrong (geocentrism, spontaneous generation, eternal universe, etc.). When evidence and logic point one direction but consensus points another, follow the evidence and logic.

## NobleMind Press Writing Principles

For The God Who Showed Up and all NobleMind writing: move slowly, carefully, prayerfully. Prioritize quality over speed 100%. Ensure right heart, honesty in Scripture handling, and double-check all Hebrew/Greek exegesis. Draft content in conversation for review before producing final files.

### Voice and Tone
- Direct, warm, older-brother tone
- Never preachy or condescending
- Sentences vary in length and rhythm — short fragments land hard after longer passages
- The prose breathes
- Exegesis feels like discovery, not a classroom exercise
- Honest when a text is difficult or when the answer is uncertain
- Hebrew/Greek explained when it matters, but never for show

## The Mission

This tool exists to help Bible students:

1. **Create and organize** Bible study materials
2. **Examine the Scriptures** systematically and carefully
3. **Verify what is taught** against the word of God
4. **Grow in knowledge** of God's will

It is offered **freely** to individuals and congregations, reflecting the principle that God's word and the tools to study it should be accessible to all.

*"Freely you received, freely give."* — Matthew 10:8
