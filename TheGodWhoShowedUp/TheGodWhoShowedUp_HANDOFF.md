## CRITICAL WRITING PRINCIPLES — ESTABLISHED ACROSS ALL SESSIONS

### 1. No man-made doctrinal labels
Do NOT use terms like "Trinity," "Tetragrammaton," "theophany," "Christophany," "Incarnation," or other labels that men invented to describe biblical concepts. Let Scripture reveal what it reveals. The text is sufficient.

### 2. No man-made labels for Hebrew/Greek terms either
Describe what the Hebrew or Greek says; don't label it with scholarly jargon the reader won't know and the Bible never uses.

### 3. Quality over speed — 100%
Move slowly, carefully, prayerfully. Ensure the right heart, honesty in handling Scripture, and double-check all Hebrew/Greek exegesis. Draft content in conversation for Paul and Pam's review before producing final files.

### 4. Let the text speak
When the text says something interesting, stop and notice it. Don't rush past details. But also don't build doctrines on observations the text doesn't explain. Present what the text says, ask the reader to consider it, and trust them.

### 5. Denominational neutrality
No Reformed lens, no charismatic lens, no Catholic lens. Just the text, the context, the cross-references. Use "preacher" or "evangelist" rather than denominational titles. Let Scripture define its own terms. "Christmas" is a man-made term and holiday not found in Scripture — the book discusses the arrival of God in the flesh, not a holiday.

### 6. Internal consistency in claims
If you establish that a person did NOT do something (e.g., Hagar was not crying out to God), do not later phrase something in a way that implies they did. Readers — and especially critical readers — will catch the contradiction.

### 7. No denominational use of "church"
The word "church" (ekklesia) in Scripture refers to the body of Christ, not a building. Do not use it to mean a building or a gathering place. Use "room," "assembly," or other appropriate terms when referring to a physical space.

### 8. Scripture interprets Scripture — no commentaries
All cross-references and interpretive connections must come from Scripture itself. Do not import conclusions from commentaries, systematic theologies, or scholarly consensus. When the Bible explains itself (e.g., Hebrews 11 explaining Abraham's reasoning), say so explicitly: "This is not commentary. This is Scripture interpreting Scripture."

### 9. Etymology from usage, not speculation
When a Hebrew word's root is debated (as with Shaddai), do not pick a root and present it as settled. Instead, let the usage pattern across Scripture define the meaning. What the text consistently shows is more reliable than what linguists argue about.

### 10. Do not claim what the text does not say
Even if a statement sounds powerful or poetic, do not present it as fact if the text does not support it. Example: "Slaves do not sing" was corrected because it is not true and the text does not say whether Israel sang in Egypt. If the text is silent on a point, we are silent on it — or we say plainly that the text does not tell us.

### 11. Present observations and threads without pushing conclusions
When Scripture preserves a detail or a thread (e.g., the seh/ayil distinction, the Moriah/temple connection), present the facts, show the cross-references, and let the reader trace the thread. Do not use leading questions or rhetorical framing that steers the reader toward a conclusion the text does not explicitly draw.

### 12. NASB verification step
Before producing final .docx and .md files for any chapter, verify all NASB quotations against Paul's downloaded NASB text file using Claude Code. This is a standard step in the pipeline for every chapter.

## KEY THEOLOGICAL DECISIONS MADE

1. **Galatians 6:16** — Present what we believe (Christians = the Israel of God), explain why from the text, acknowledge the alternative reading of kai, let the reader decide. Framework does not depend on this single verse.

2. **Elohim plural form** — State the grammatical fact. Note it is suggestive. Do NOT use the word "Trinity." Say Scripture reveals the Father, the Son, and the Spirit as one God, progressively, through the unfolding of the biblical story.

3. **The sandals at the burning bush** — Command appears twice in Scripture (Exodus 3 and Joshua 5:15). Both are promise-fulfillment thresholds. The observation is raised as a question, not a doctrine.

4. **Sandals and sonship observation** — Used in the CONCLUSION: Sandals come off when approaching the holy God (Exodus 3, Joshua 5). Sandals go back on when the Father restores the son (Luke 15:22). The shift from servant posture to son/daughter restoration — the change the torn veil accomplished.

5. **Chapter ordering** — Chapter 1 follows "big picture first" (Elohim). Chapters 2+ follow chronological order. Yahweh moves to Chapter 5 as the hinge between patriarchal era and Exodus, consistent with Exodus 6:2–3. Paul and Pam confirmed this reordering on March 23, 2026.

6. **Hagar and El Roi** — Hagar was not specifically calling out to the God of Abram; God sought her. The name Ishmael ("God hears") refers to God giving heed to her affliction, not to a prayer she directed at Him. Phrasing must not imply she cried out to God when the text does not say that.

7. **Etymology of Shaddai** — Do not pick a debated root and present it as settled. Let usage pattern across Scripture define meaning. Every appearance ties to overwhelming power overriding natural impossibility.

8. **Lamb vs. Ram on Moriah** — Abraham used the standard word for a sacrifice animal (seh), likely reassuring his son while trusting God would resolve the impossible through resurrection. God provided a ram (ayil — different Hebrew word). The distinction is real. John 1:29 calls Jesus the Lamb. The thread is there — Scripture draws it. We present it without pushing a prophetic reading of Abraham's words. Revised March 23, 2026 — removed the leading question "Was Abraham wrong? Or was he speaking a truth larger than the moment?"

9. **Moriah / Temple connection** — 2 Chronicles 3:1 explicitly locates the temple on Mount Moriah. The text makes the connection; we follow it.

10. **Red Sea crossing in Chapter 6** — Given full weight as the defining act of deliverance, not reduced to background summary. The 1 Corinthians 10:1–2 baptism connection is made at the crossing point. The Song of Moses noted as the first recorded worship of the delivered nation (not "first song" — we do not know whether Israel sang in Egypt).

11. **Conditional structure of Exodus 15:26** — The name Rapha is unconditional (God IS the healer). But the specific promise of protection from Egypt's diseases was framed within the covenant — "if you give earnest heed." Read it the way God said it, not reshaped into something He did not say.

12. **Amalek as shadow** — Presented as pattern within the established 1 Corinthians 10 framework, not as dogmatic type. Deuteronomy 25:17–18 provides the detail (attacks the stragglers, the weak, the weary). "From generation to generation" traced through Scripture. The text does not say "Amalek represents sin" — the pattern speaks for itself within the framework.

13. **The tree at Marah** — Text does not explain what kind of tree or how it worked. We do not invent an explanation. What the text tells us is that God showed Moses the tree. The solution was revealed, not discovered.

14. **The angel of the Lord in Judges 6** — The text calls the figure "the angel of the Lord" (vv. 11–12) but shifts to "the Lord" (v. 14) without explanation or transition. Present what the text shows without man-made labels like "theophany" or "Christophany." Let the reader see what the text shows.

15. **Gideon's honest question** — "If the Lord is with us, why has this happened?" is treated with respect, not rebuke. The text does not rebuke it. God's answer is a commission, not an explanation. The prophet already gave the diagnosis (6:7–10).

16. **Pronoun shift in Psalm 23** — Verses 1–3 use third person ("He"); verse 4 shifts to second person ("You") in the valley. When it gets darkest, the language gets most intimate. Observation from the text, not imposed.

17. **Radaph in Psalm 23:6** — "Follow" is the Hebrew word for "pursue/chase," used elsewhere for enemies in battle. Goodness and lovingkindness are chasing David down. The word is well-attested; the observation is from the text.

18. **Zedekiah / Tsidkenu wordplay** — The king's name (Tsidqiyyahu = "The Lord is my righteousness") vs. the promised King's name (Yahweh Tsidqenu = "The Lord our righteousness"). The irony is in the text. The shift from "my" (personal, unsustainable claim) to "our" (communal gift) is the point.

19. **Shalom → Tsidkenu connection** — Shalom said "you will not die" but did not explain the basis. Tsidkenu provides the basis: His righteousness, not ours, is what allows us to stand before a holy God.

20. **Ezekiel's temple vision (Chapters 40–48)** — The detailed vision is debated among faithful students. We do not attempt to settle the discussion. What matters is what happens inside the vision: the glory returns and God declares He will dwell among His people forever. The details of the vision are not the point; the presence is.

21. **The almah / virgin in Isaiah 7:14** — The Hebrew word almah refers to a young woman of marriageable age. Matthew uses a Greek word that specifically means virgin and applies the passage directly to the birth of Jesus (Matthew 1:22–23). We do not wade into the scholarly debate. Matthew settles the matter by how he applies it. Scripture interprets Scripture.

22. **Jesus vs. Immanuel** — Jesus (Yeshua) is the name on the birth record. Immanuel is who He is. Every name in the book has worked this way — not merely a label, but a revelation of character.

23. **No "Christmas" or "Incarnation"** — Both are man-made terms. "Christmas" is a holiday not found in Scripture, not celebrated by the early church. "Incarnation" is a theological label. We describe what happened: God took on flesh, came in human form, dwelt among us. The text's own language is sufficient. Jesus gave us the bread and fruit of the vine to remember Him by — the cost of redemption.
