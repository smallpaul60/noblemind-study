## What Was Accomplished This Session

### 1. Chapter 4 Written: Leaves Without Fruit — Nisan 11

Covers the fig tree cursing and the temple cleansing. Structure:

- Mark 11:12 — "On the next day" after the entry = Sunday, Nisan 11
- Fig tree cursed (Mark 11:12–14): leaves but no fruit. The parallel to fruitless religion is noted as Mark's placement, not stated as textual claim. "We are free to notice the connection. We are not free to claim the text makes it explicit."
- Temple cleansed (Mark 11:15–17): merchants operating on a working day (supports inclusive count / Saturday entry). Isaiah 56:7 and Jeremiah 7:11 quoted by Jesus — God's own words applied to what He found.
- Chief priests and scribes begin seeking to destroy Him (Mark 11:18)
- Matthew and Luke compression addressed: literary arrangement, not chronological contradiction. Mark provides the time markers; we follow the account that gives the sequence.
- Matthew's fig tree compression (cursing + withering in one scene) also addressed.

### 2. Exodus 12 Keeping Period — Rule Reinforced

Paul caught that Chapter 4's original closing slipped back into "examination" language as if Exodus 12 commanded post-selection inspection. It does not. Exodus says select an unblemished lamb and keep it until the fourteenth. The *why* of the four-day keeping period is never stated. Corrected to clearly separate Gospel fact (Jesus was questioned, no fault found) from Exodus observation (the lamb was kept four days). This correction was applied consistently through all remaining chapters this session.

**The established rule:** Exodus 12 requires the lamb to be unblemished and kept for four days. The text does not say the keeping period was for inspection. The correspondence between the keeping period and the Gospel examinations is our observation, not the text's statement. We note it as remarkable and leave it to the reader.

### 3. Chapter 5 Written: The Lamb Is Examined — Nisan 12

The longest day of teaching. Structure:

- Mark 11:20 — fig tree withered "in the morning"
- Four confrontations, each with its own section:
  - Chief priests & elders: authority question — could not answer Jesus' counter-question (Mark 11:27–33)
  - Pharisees & Herodians: tax trap — "Render to Caesar..." (Mark 12:13–17)
  - Sadducees: resurrection trick — "You are greatly mistaken" (Mark 12:18–27)
  - A scribe: greatest commandment — sincere question, "You are not far from the kingdom" (Mark 12:28–34)
- Matthew 22:46 — "No one was able to answer Him a word, nor did anyone dare from that day on to ask Him another question"
- Mark 14:1 / Matthew 26:2 — "two days before" / "after two days" — time anchor confirming Monday, Nisan 12
- Luke 19:47 — "teaching daily" — confirms multiple days
- Olivet Discourse acknowledged but not unpacked (beyond this book's scope)
- Closing: Exodus 12 parallel handled with established care — Gospel examinations are fact, connection to keeping period is observation

### 4. Chapter 6 Written: The Anointing and the Betrayal — Nisan 12–13

The contrast chapter. Structure:

- Anointing at Bethany (Matthew 26:6–13; Mark 14:3–9) — Simon the leper's house
- Two-anointing question addressed honestly: John 12:1–8 (Lazarus's house, feet, before entry) vs. Matthew/Mark (Simon's house, head, after "two days before"). Both readings presented — two events or one arranged topically. No forced harmonization. "Neither creates a conflict with the timeline."
- "She has anointed My body beforehand for the burial" (Mark 14:8) — Jesus knows what is coming
- Judas goes to chief priests (Mark 14:10–11; Matthew 26:14–16; Luke 22:3–6)
- Thirty pieces of silver — connected to Exodus 21:32 (price of a slave) as legal background. Zechariah 11:12 deliberately NOT cited here; Matthew's own prophetic citation comes later in the narrative (Matt 27:9) and has its own textual questions.
- "Satan entered into Judas" (Luke 22:3) — stated without speculation
- The quiet day (Nisan 13) — brief transition to the Last Supper

### 5. Chapter 7 Written: The Passover — Nisan 14 Begins (Tuesday Evening)

The Last Supper chapter. Structure:

- Preparation: man with water pitcher, upper room (Mark 14:13–16)
- "I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer" (Luke 22:15–16)
- Foot washing (John 13:3–15) — placed before the bread and cup per John's narrative
- The bread and cup (Matthew 26:26; Mark 14:23–24; Luke 22:19–20)
- Betrayer identified (John 13:21–30) — "And it was night" left without commentary
- **The John timing question** — both readings presented fairly:
  - Reading 1: "eat the Passover" = broader festival meals (possible, but requires the phrase to mean something other than its most natural sense)
  - Reading 2: Jesus ate the Passover early because He would be dead before the national observance (more consistent with the pattern, labeled as inference)
  - **Critical clarification added per Paul's correction:** The tension between John and the Synoptics is the same regardless of Wednesday or Friday. This is a Synoptic-vs-John question, not a Wednesday-vs-Friday question. It does not affect the day-of-the-week evidence.

### 6. Chapter 8 Written: The Cup and the Trials — Nisan 14 (Night and Morning)

The longest night. Gethsemane through the final verdict. Structure:

- **Gethsemane:** Three prayers. The cup = suffering and death (grounded in Mark 10:38 where James and John were told they would drink it, and did). What else the cup encompassed is not stated in the text — acknowledged honestly. Luke 22:44 — sweat like drops of blood.
- **The arrest:** Judas's kiss. John 18:4–6 — the crowd fell backward. Peter's sword — twelve legions of angels available, not called. Disciples fled (Matt 26:56).
- **Annas** (John 18:12–24): No formal charge.
- **Caiaphas & Sanhedrin** (Mark 14:55–64): False witnesses inconsistent. Condemned for claiming to be the Son of God — "They condemned Him for telling the truth."
- **Peter's denial** (Matt 26:69–75; Luke 22:61–62): Three denials. "The Lord turned and looked at Peter." No description of the expression — Luke didn't give one.
- **Pilate** (Mark 15:1–5; Luke 23:1–5): "I find no guilt in this man" — first of three declarations. Jesus' silence amazed Pilate. **Isaiah 53:7 placed here** — during the silence before accusers, not at the arrest where Jesus did speak.
- **Herod** (Luke 23:6–12): Questioned at length. Answered nothing. No charge.
- **Pilate again** (Luke 23:13–25; John 19:4): Second and third "no guilt" declarations. Barabbas released. Pilate hands Him over.
- **Closing:** Every examiner — religious and civil — had the opportunity to find fault. None did. Exodus 12:5 noted as background. "The Lamb was without blemish. Not because we say so. Because His enemies said so."

### 7. Epistles Discussion

Paul asked whether Hebrews or other epistles add weight to the Wednesday-vs-Friday question. Conclusion: No. Every relevant epistle passage (Hebrews 5:7; 13:12; 1 Peter 2:22–24; 1 Corinthians 11:23–26; 15:3–8) enriches meaning or confirms Gospel details, but none bears on the day question. The Wednesday case stands entirely on Gospel evidence. If a passage doesn't move the needle on the question the book is answering, it doesn't earn its place.

Exception: 1 Corinthians 5:7 ("Christ our Passover also has been sacrificed") is already in Chapter 1 — supports the typological framework.

## CRITICAL RULE — Textual Integrity (Unchanged)

Do not state things as fact that the text does not explicitly say. Do not embellish God's story.

- Where the text is explicit, say so.
- Where a conclusion requires inference, identify it as inference.
- Where we do not know, say we do not know.
- Where other Scripture makes a connection, let THAT Scripture make it and cite it.
- The standard: If someone opens a Bible and reads the passage being cited, will they find what we said?

## CRITICAL RULE — Exodus 12 Keeping Period (Reinforced This Session)

Exodus 12 says: select an unblemished lamb on Nisan 10, keep it until Nisan 14. The text does NOT say the keeping period was for inspection or examination. The correspondence between the four-day keeping period and the Gospel examinations is our observation — remarkable, worth noting — but it is not the text's explicit statement. Every chapter must maintain this distinction.
