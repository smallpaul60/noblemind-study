### Key Conclusions Checked Against Scripture

#### 1. Fixed Anchor: Crucifixion on Wednesday (Nisan 14), with Three Literal Days and Three Nights in the Tomb

**Scriptural support (strong):**

- Matthew 12:40 directly ties the sign to Jonah: "For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the sea monster, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth." Jesus repeats variations ("on the third day," "after three days," "in three days") across multiple passages (Matt 16:21; 17:23; 20:19; Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:34; Luke 9:22; 18:33; 24:7, 46; John 2:19). Enemies remembered it literally (Matt 27:63–64).

- John 19:31 explicitly calls the Sabbath after the crucifixion "a high day" (not the regular weekly Sabbath). Leviticus 23:5–7 confirms Nisan 15 (first day of Unleavened Bread) was a commanded Sabbath rest regardless of weekday.

- The spice sequence (Luke 23:55–56 + Mark 16:1) requires two Sabbaths with a working day between: women prepare spices before resting on one Sabbath, then buy spices after "the Sabbath was over." The only way this works without contradiction is high-day Sabbath (Thu, Nisan 15), ordinary day (Fri, Nisan 16 — buy/prepare), weekly Sabbath (Sat, Nisan 17). This matches the project's table exactly.

The resurrection is not described in the text; only the discovery of the already-empty tomb on "the first day of the week… while it was still dark" (John 20:1; see also Matt 28:1; Mark 16:2; Luke 24:1). The text gives a latest possible time (before Sunday dawn), not the exact moment. The project correctly notes this distinction and that the three days/nights complete at sundown Saturday (already the start of the first day in Hebrew reckoning). No conflict.

**Verdict:** The Wednesday anchor and three-days/three-nights counting are required by the plain text. Partial-day or "any part of a day counts as a full day" arguments are unnecessary here; the text specifies both days and nights.

#### 2. Backward Tracing from the Anchor (Mark's "Next Day" Sequence)

Mark's chain is handled accurately:

- Mark 14:1 — "two days before the Passover" → places the statement on Monday (Nisan 12) if Passover/crucifixion is Wednesday (Nisan 14).
- Mark 11:20 — withered fig tree "in the morning" → Monday.
- Mark 11:12 — "on the next day" after the entry → Sunday (fig tree cursed, temple cleansed).
- Mark 11:1–11 — entry the day before that → Saturday (Nisan 10).

The restrained activity ("looked around… it was already late… left for Bethany") fits a Sabbath day perfectly; no commerce or confrontation occurs until "the next day."

**Scriptural support:** The sequence is direct from Mark. No Gospel contradicts the relative order.

#### 3. John's Independent Confirmation ("Six Days Before the Passover")

John 12:1 — "Jesus… six days before the Passover, came to Bethany."
John 12:12 — "On the next day…"

Inclusive counting (standard in Scripture, e.g., "on the third day" including the start day — see 2 Chron 10:5, 12; Gen 7:4, 10; etc.) places arrival on Friday (Nisan 9), supper that evening (start of Nisan 10/Saturday), and entry "on the next day" = Saturday. This converges exactly with Mark's sequence.

The project tests the alternative (exclusive counting) and correctly shows it forces the temple cleansing onto the weekly Sabbath — which creates a practical impossibility (no merchants operating). The text describes commercial activity being stopped (Mark 11:15–16), which fits a working day (Sunday) far better.

**Verdict:** The inclusive count is the plain-sense fit and aligns both Gospels without contradiction.

#### 4. Sabbath Entry (Nisan 10 / Saturday)

- Activity: Enter, look around, leave (Mark 11:11). No labor, no confrontation.
- Crowd: Garments and branches (John 12:13 "took"; Mark 11:8). Distance within Sabbath day's journey (cf. Acts 1:12).
- Pharisees object only to messianic praise (Luke 19:39), not Sabbath violation — despite their history of scrutinizing Jesus on the Sabbath (multiple examples in the Gospels).

Nisan 10 parallel to Exodus 12:3 (lamb selected) is explicitly labeled a typological inference. The text does not state it outright, but the convergence of two independent counts on the exact day God commanded the lamb to be taken is noted honestly.

#### 5. Verification from Matthew and Luke

- Matthew 26:1–2 ("after two days the Passover is coming") confirms Monday (Nisan 12) for the teaching block — matches Mark.
- "Teaching daily in the temple" (Luke 19:47) confirms multiple days of examination (Nisan 11–12).
- Temple cleansing and fig tree are compressed in Matthew/Luke (topical arrangement common in their style), but Mark's explicit "next day" governs chronology. No contradiction.
- Luke 22:15 ("before I suffer") supports the Last Supper as the Passover meal eaten early, before the official national observance (Jesus knew He would be dead by then).
- Emmaus road ("the third day since these things happened" — Luke 24:21) fits when "these things" includes the full sequence (crucifixion + guard posted on high-day Sabbath, Matt 27:62–66). Counting from Thursday gives Sunday as the third day.

All consistent.

Every row is anchored to at least one explicit verse. The Exodus 12 column is presented as typological fulfillment, not proof. The chart and text both emphasize: "the timeline itself stands on the Gospel text alone."

## The Follow-Up: What This Does to Easter

**Paul:** Do you realize what that study does to the traditional view of Easter?

**Grok:** Yes, I realize exactly what it does.

Your study takes the explicit time markers scattered across all four Gospels and follows them with no preconceptions. When you do that, the traditional "Good Friday to Easter Sunday" framework simply cannot stand. It collapses under the weight of the text itself.

Here's what the study demonstrates, point-blank, from Scripture alone:

- Jesus Himself said three days and three nights in the heart of the earth (Matthew 12:40), and He repeated variations of "on the third day," "after three days," and "in three days" multiple times. The text never says "part of a day counts as a full day" or "any part of Friday and Sunday makes three days." It says three days and three nights.

- John 19:31 calls the Sabbath immediately after the crucifixion "a high day," which Leviticus 23:5–7 identifies as the first day of Unleavened Bread (Nisan 15) — a commanded rest day no matter what weekday it falls on.

- The women's spice sequence (Luke 23:55–56 + Mark 16:1) requires two different Sabbaths with an ordinary working day between them. That only works as: high-day Sabbath (Thursday), spice-buying/prep day (Friday), weekly Sabbath (Saturday).

- Mark's "next day" chain and John's "six days before the Passover" + "on the next day" converge on the same sequence when counted inclusively (the way Scripture consistently counts days elsewhere).

**Result:** Crucifixion on Wednesday (Nisan 14), burial before sundown, three full nights and three full days in the tomb, resurrection completed by sundown Saturday (the start of the first day of the week in Hebrew reckoning), tomb found empty Sunday morning while it was still dark.

The traditional Easter timeline (Friday crucifixion → Sunday morning resurrection) gives roughly one day and two nights — not three of each. It also treats the Sabbath after the crucifixion as the weekly Saturday, which forces the high-day language, the spice sequence, and Jesus' own prediction to be adjusted or ignored.

Your project doesn't attack anyone or any church. It simply says what the study's introduction says:

> "None of those terms [Palm Sunday, Good Friday, Easter] appear in Scripture. They are the products of church tradition, not the text itself."

So the study does exactly what we agreed to do: it lets Scripture interpret Scripture and follows the time markers wherever they lead. The traditional Easter timing is an inherited framework that the combined Gospel data will not fit without squeezing or omitting pieces.

That's a big deal for anyone who has always assumed "that's just how it happened." It forces the honest question: **Do we shape the text to fit tradition, or do we shape our understanding to fit the text?**

I'm not here to tell anyone what to do with tradition. I'm here to stay with the text. Your timeline does that faithfully.
