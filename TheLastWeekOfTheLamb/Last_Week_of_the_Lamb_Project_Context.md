## What Has Been Established

### The Three Days and Three Nights Argument

Jesus said He would be "three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (Matthew 12:40), tied directly to Jonah 1:17. He also said "on the third day," "after three days," and "in three days" at various points. The Pharisees took it literally (Matthew 27:63–64).

The traditional "Good Friday" crucifixion does not work — Friday afternoon to Sunday dawn is at most two nights and one full day. The partial-day argument ("any part of a day counts") ignores the "three nights" half of the statement.

**The textual evidence for two Sabbaths:**
- John 19:31 — the Sabbath after the crucifixion was "a high day" (not the regular weekly Sabbath)
- Leviticus 23:5–7 — the first day of Unleavened Bread (Nisan 15) was a commanded Sabbath rest regardless of the day of the week
- The Luke/Mark spice sequence: Luke 23:56 says the women prepared spices BEFORE resting on the Sabbath. Mark 16:1 says they BOUGHT spices AFTER the Sabbath was over. You cannot prepare what you haven't purchased — unless there were two Sabbaths with a working day between them.

**Conclusion reached:** The text supports a crucifixion earlier in the week than Friday, with two Sabbaths (a high-day feast Sabbath and the regular weekly Sabbath) and a working day between them.

### The Passover Lamb Typology (Exodus 12)

The parallels are precise:
- Lamb selected Nisan 10 → Jesus enters Jerusalem on Nisan 10
- Lamb inspected for four days (10th through 13th) → Jesus examined by every authority group for four days — no fault found
- Lamb must be without blemish → "I find no guilt in this man" (Luke 23:4)
- Lamb killed Nisan 14 "at twilight" (between the evenings, afternoon) → Jesus dies ~3 PM on Nisan 14
- Lamb's blood applied to doorposts → Jesus' blood poured out for forgiveness of sins

### The John Timing Discrepancy

**The tension:** The Synoptics clearly identify the Last Supper as a Passover meal (Mark 14:12, Luke 22:15). But John 18:28 says the Jewish leaders had not yet eaten the Passover the morning after the arrest, and John 19:14 calls the crucifixion day "the day of preparation for the Passover."

**Our conclusion (Option 2):** Jesus deliberately ate the Passover with His disciples a day ahead of the official national observance, because He knew He would be dead by then. He could not simultaneously eat the Passover AND be the Passover sacrifice. This reading is the most consistent with the entire Old Covenant system of types and shadows fulfilled in the New Testament.

Support: Luke 22:15–16 — "I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you BEFORE I suffer" — suggests urgency and awareness that this is happening ahead of the normal schedule.

### "Palm Sunday" Is Tradition, Not Text

The text never identifies the day of the week for the Triumphal Entry. "Palm Sunday" comes from church tradition, likely solidified at or after the Council of Nicaea (325 AD). The text gives us Nisan dates and relative time markers ("the next day"), not days of the week. The only explicitly named day of the week in all four Gospels is "the first day of the week" for the resurrection (Sunday).

### The "Good Friday" / "Easter" Problem

Similarly, "Good Friday" and "Easter" are tradition-based designations, not textual ones. The text says "the day of preparation" before a Sabbath that was "a high day." Tradition assumed this meant Friday. The text does not say Friday.

### The Entry on the Sabbath — RESOLVED

Both Mark's "next day" sequence (traced backward from the Wednesday crucifixion) and John's "six days before the Passover" (counted inclusively) independently place the Triumphal Entry on Saturday, Nisan 10.

**Why the Sabbath entry works:**
- Mark 11:11 — Jesus entered, looked around, and left. No teaching, no confrontation, no cleansing. Remarkably restrained — consistent with Sabbath behavior.
- The action begins "the next day" (Mark 11:12) — Sunday — when the merchants were operating.
- The Pharisees objected only to messianic praise (Luke 19:39), not Sabbath violation — despite their history of scrutinizing Jesus on the Sabbath.
- Bethany to Jerusalem was within a Sabbath day's journey (Acts 1:12).
- Riding a donkey was not considered labor.
- The crowd's actions (spreading garments, taking branches) were their actions, not His.

**Why the exclusive count fails:** If Jesus arrived in Bethany on Thursday (Nisan 8) and entered on Friday (Nisan 9), the temple cleansing ("the next day" — Mark 11:12) would fall on Saturday, the weekly Sabbath. The merchants would not have been operating. The inclusive count avoids this entirely.

The Nisan 10 / Exodus 12:3 lamb-selection connection is a typological inference, not an explicit statement. But the convergence of two independent counts on the exact day God appointed for the lamb to be selected is noted honestly.

### The Year of the Crucifixion — AD 31

The astronomical investigation (*When Did the Lamb Die?*) examined every year in the AD 27–34 window for when Nisan 14 fell on a Wednesday. Five textual constraints were applied: Pilate in office (AD 26–36), John the Baptist already preaching (no earlier than AD 28–29), at least three Passovers during Jesus' ministry, Nisan 14 on a Wednesday, and early enough to precede Paul's conversion (~AD 33–34).

**Result:** AD 31 is the only year in the plausible window where Nisan 14 probably falls on a Wednesday and all textual constraints are satisfied.

**Honest limitation:** The first-century Jewish calendar was observational (based on crescent moon sightings), not calculated. This introduces an irreducible ±1 day uncertainty. AD 31 is the strongest candidate, not an absolute certainty.

**Key insight:** Nearly all prior astronomical scholarship (Humphreys & Waddington, Newton, Finegan) solved for a Friday crucifixion — the right day of the month (Nisan 14) but the wrong day of the week. When the same data is examined for a Wednesday, AD 30 and AD 33 are eliminated and AD 31 emerges.
